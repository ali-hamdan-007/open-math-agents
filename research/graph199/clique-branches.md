# Clique-separator extensions for Graph 199

## Uniform attachments and arbitrary branches

Let \(Q\) be a clique separator of a \(k\)-connected graph \(G\), and assume that for each component \(C_i\) of \(G-Q\), each \(q\in Q\) is complete or anticomplete to \(C_i\). Choose a maximum independent set of each \(C_i\) and retain it along with \(Q\), giving an induced split graph \(G'\). Every representative of \(C_i\) has at least \(k\) neighbors in \(Q\), since deleting \(N_Q(C_i)\) would otherwise separate that entire component from another. Also \(|Q|\ge k\). Thus \(G'\) is \(k\)-connected and inherits the induced-tree upper bound.

A minimum path cover of any graph \(H\) has at most \(\alpha(H)\) paths. Otherwise choosing one endpoint from each of the paths would give a larger independent set, since endpoints from distinct paths cannot be adjacent without merging the two paths. Split some paths if needed to obtain a cover with exactly \(\alpha(C_i)\) paths in \(C_i\). Any spanning path of \(G'\) lifts by substituting one separate path of this cover for each representative. The complete attachments ensure that the edges into and out of that path are available. In particular the earlier split result at \(k=3\) extends to arbitrary branches when the attachment of every port to each branch is uniform.

## Nonuniform clique branches at connectivity three

Let \(G\) be a 3-connected graph with no induced tree on six vertices. Suppose a clique \(Q\) separates clique components \(C_i\), and each \(C_i\) has a representative \(r_i\) adjacent to every \(q\in Q\) that has a neighbor in \(C_i\). Assume that one vertex \(u\in Q\) sees exactly four representatives, called the four core branches. Its row is full because no vertex can see five representatives without inducing a six-vertex star. If there are at least four other branches, then \(G\) has a spanning path.

### Restrictions from the induced-tree bound

A port is a vertex of \(Q\). Say a port is good for a branch if it has at least two neighbors in that branch, or if the branch is a singleton and the port sees it. The port \(u\) is complete to each core branch. If it missed some vertex of such a branch, that vertex and the four representatives together with \(u\) would give an induced tree on six vertices.

A port \(q\) seeing any branch outside the four core branches sees only one such outsider, and it sees at least two core branches. Indeed the edge \(uq\) together with all representatives seen by just one endpoint is an induced double star. It has at most three leaves. As \(u\) sees four core branches and no outsiders, and \(q\) sees at most four branches altogether, those bounds give the claim. Thus the port sets \(Q_j\) meeting the outsider branches are disjoint. Each has at least three ports by connectivity. A nonsingleton outsider has at least two good ports. If it had at most one good port, removing its representative and that single port if present would separate all remaining members of the branch from the rest of the graph.

At a port \(q\) in an outsider group, let \(t\in\{2,3\}\) be the number of core branches it sees. The edge \(uq\) already has \(5-t\) private representative leaves. For any one of its \(t\) touched core branches to which \(q\) is incomplete, choose a vertex not seen by \(q\). It is another private leaf of \(u\), and these added leaves come from disjoint branches. Thus the number of incomplete touched core branches is at most \(t-2\), and \(q\) is complete to at least two core branches. In particular it is good for at least two.

For ports \(q,q'\) in distinct outsider groups, their sets of good core branches differ in at most one label. They already have two distinct private outsider leaves. If good status differs for a core branch, select a vertex of that branch adjacent to exactly one of \(q,q'\). This is possible because the good port sees at least two vertices and the other at most one, or because the branch is a singleton. Differences on two core branches would give two further private leaves, yielding an induced six-vertex double star. Consequently at most one pair of core labels can be missed by all good incidences in some outsider group. For any group missing a pair, every row in that group is good for exactly its complement. Two groups missing different pairs would violate the bound on the symmetric difference. This reasoning also applies when using only a nonempty subset of the ports in one group.

### Assigning ports

For at least five outsider groups, regard each group as one resource from which we may choose a port for one core demand. Treat each port outside those groups as a separate resource. If exactly four groups exist and a nongroup port other than \(u\) meets a core branch, use the same resources. If exactly four groups exist, no such nongroup port exists, and some group has at least four ports, reserve two of its ports including one good for its outsider. Use two other actual ports from this group as two separate resources. All unmodified group resources may be matched to a demand as long as at least one of their ports satisfies it.

The remaining case at exactly four groups has only groups of three and \(u\) as the only nongroup port meeting a core branch. This is impossible already at the level of representative incidence. The core masks of rows in distinct groups differ in at most one label, by the same private-leaf double-star count, and each mask has size two or three. If all masks have size two, they all coincide across groups, so a core representative misses every port except \(u\). Otherwise choose a size-three row \(P\) in some group \(Q_s\), missing core label \(a\). Every outside-group row is a subset of \(P\), so both other rows of \(Q_s\) must meet \(a\) for its representative to have degree at least three. An outside-group row cannot equal \(P\), as it would differ by at least two from a row of size two or three containing \(a\). Hence take an outside-group pair \(R\subseteq P\); both rows of \(Q_s\) that meet \(a\) must equal \(R\cup\{a\}\). Now any outside-group mask within distance one of both triples \(P\) and \(R\cup\{a\}\) must equal their intersection \(R\). The unique label of \(P\setminus R\) then has only two ports, including \(u\), contradicting the degree of its representative.

We now have at least four outsider groups and at least six resources meeting the demands as a whole. Choose the exceptional pair of core labels missed by a group under the selected resources, if it exists, as the two endpoint branches. Otherwise choose arbitrary two endpoints. Give each of the four core branches one ordinary demand, and each of the two nonendpoint core branches one additional good demand. A resource is adjacent to a demand if it can supply a port of the indicated kind.

Hall's inequalities follow by counting the distinct core labels represented by a demand subset. One label has at most two demands, and can use \(u\) and another resource for its ordinary demand if two are present. That other resource exists since the branch representative has at least three neighbors in \(Q\). If two labels form the exceptional pair then both demands are ordinary and the same two resources suffice. If the pair is not exceptional, every group offers some good port for at least one of the demanded labels. This gives at least five resources including \(u\), enough for at most four demands. With three or more labels each group again offers a good port, since each of its retained rows has at least two good labels. This is enough for any subset of at most five demands. The whole set of six demands reaches at least six resources by the construction. In the just-four-groups case with an extra nongroup port it serves at least one ordinary demand. In the case with two actual resources from a modified group, both have at least one suitable ordinary demand.

Use the six assigned ports for core branches. Any unmodified outsider group still has at least two ports after at most one port is assigned to a core branch, and at least one good port remains. Use two distinct ports, including a good port for nonsingleton outsiders. The modified group uses its reserved pair.

An interior branch with two ports can choose distinct internal ends: use the common representative at the ordinary port and another adjacent vertex at the good port. For a singleton use its sole vertex between the distinct ports. A clique has a spanning path between any two distinct vertices. Each of the designated endpoint branches instead lists its clique with the representative next to its sole port. The resulting blocks are vertex-disjoint and can be concatenated through their ends in \(Q\), placing the two endpoint blocks first and last. All unused ports are singleton blocks. This gives the desired spanning path.

This result uses the full row and four outsider branches. It is independent of the finite pipeline for pure split graphs in the earlier proof, and leaves fewer outsiders and graphs without a clique-branch decomposition outside its scope.
