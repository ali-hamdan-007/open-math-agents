# A separating pair with at least three components forces order at most 3k

Let G be finite, simple, 2-connected, and satisfy the even-distance hypothesis of Conjecture 189. Let k be the second-smallest degree, so every vertex has at most k other vertices at positive even distance. Suppose removing two vertices p,q leaves t≥3 components. Then |G|≤3k; consequently the independently proved order theorem yields a Hamilton path. No initial upper bound on order is assumed.

Every component contacts both p and q, since either sole anchor would separate that component from the rest. Each component containing more than one vertex has at least two distinct vertices through which it contacts the two anchors. Otherwise deleting its sole attachment vertex would disconnect it. Call a vertex high if its degree is at least k. At most one vertex is not high.

## No component consists solely of a below-k vertex

We can choose p so that it has a high neighbor in every component. This is automatic except potentially in a component that contains the below-k vertex. If that vertex were the only contact to both anchors, it would be the sole attachment of a nonsingleton component, which was excluded. Thus one anchor has a high contact there, and all its contacts in the other components are high.

Write a_i for the number of vertices in component i adjacent to p and A for their sum. Any p-neighbor in i sees all A−a_i p-neighbors in the other components at distance two, so (t−1)A≤tk. From a chosen high p-neighbor in component i, its closed neighborhood contains at least k−1 vertices of that component; only p,q lie outside. Thus at least k−1−a_i vertices there are at distance two from p. This lower bound rises by one if the chosen high neighbor misses q. Summation gives A≥t(k−1)−k. If t≥4, already at t=4 the combined bounds require 3k−4≤4k/3, impossible for k≥3; increasing t only worsens the inequality. Also t≤k+1 by a_i≥1. Hence t=3. For three components the combined bounds read

    2k−3 ≤ A ≤ floor(3k/2),

so k≤6. Equality A=2k−3 forces a high contact common to both anchors in every component, since otherwise the lower bound rises by one. Whenever some such contact exists, q itself is distance two from p if pq is absent, so equality also forces pq to be an edge. The upper bound A≤floor(3k/2) holds for either anchor without a high-contact assumption.

A p-contact that has already found k distance-two vertices in other components is adjacent to every other vertex of its own component: otherwise a shortest internal path to a missing vertex has a second vertex at distance two from the contact. If a common contact x has already found k−1 such outside witnesses and pq is an edge, at most one vertex inside its component can fail to be adjacent to x. Indeed the torso induced on its component together with p,q is 2-connected. Any path in G after one vertex deletion projects to a walk in this torso by replacing excursions through other components by the edge pq or by deleting loops. The vertices p,q are adjacent to x. Thus the distance-two layer of x in the torso has size at most one; it cannot separate x from a vertex beyond distance two. These observations will bound the vertices that p could fail to see within two steps.

- For k=6, A=9, and the three a_i are all 3 since A−a_i≤6. A high common contact in each component already sees six external p-contacts, so finds no internal distance-two witness. Every vertex outside the nine original p-contacts and the two anchors is consequently at distance two from p. The order is at most 2+9+6=17.
- For k=5, A=7 and the sizes are 2,2,3. There are high common contacts everywhere and pq is an edge. Take a common contact from each 2-part. Its five cross-component p-contacts exhaust its allowance, so no q-only contact can occur in the other components; using both choices excludes q-only contacts everywhere. In a 2-part a common contact is adjacent to the entire component, and in the 3-part a common contact has at most one internal nonneighbor by the torso observation. Thus at most one nonport fails to lie at distance two from p, and n≤2+7+5+1=15.
- For k=4 and A=6, the sizes are 2,2,2; every p-contact exhausts its four witnesses in the other components and is adjacent to the entirety of its own component. Thus n≤2+6+4=12. For A=5 a high common contact exists in each component. Each component has at least k−1=3 vertices. If the sizes are 1,1,3, the common contact in each 1-part has four cross-component p-contacts, forcing every q-contact in the other components to be a p-contact. This gives a 1-part with a single total attachment, impossible. For sizes 1,2,2 the 1-part must have a q-only contact, in addition to its unique common p-contact. Any common contact in either 2-part therefore sees three cross-component p-contacts and the additional q-only contact, for four separate witnesses. The common contacts of all three parts are now adjacent to their entire components. Hence n≤2+5+4=11.
- For k=3, every component has at least two vertices. If A=3 then each part has one high common p-contact. Each part needs a second attachment, necessarily to q, giving q at least six component contacts; its upper bound is four. If A=4 the sizes are 1,1,2. At least two parts have a high common contact, since any part without one strengthens the lower bound A≥3 by one. Thus one 1-part has a common contact; its three cross-component p-contacts exhaust its cap. In the other 1-part all q-contacts must consequently be p-contacts. This forces its only p-contact to be its sole total attachment, impossible.
- For k=2, each anchor has exactly one contact per component, and each p-contact exhausts its two slots on the other components. It sees every vertex of its own component. If one contact is common, it forces all contacts in the other components to be common as well. Then every component has only one attachment and is therefore a singleton. The order is five. If none is common, the three q-contacts each lie at distance two from p through their respective p-contacts, contradiction. The case k<2 is impossible in a 2-connected graph of this order.

## A component consists of the single below-k vertex z

Here z must see p and q, and k≥3. Every other component is high, has order at least k−1≥2, and has at least two distinct attachment vertices. Write P_i for its set of vertices adjacent to p or q, and R_i for its other vertices. All P_i vertices are at distance two from z; write b=Σ|P_i|≤k. There are at least two high components, so each |P_i|≤k−2. Thus every R_i is nonempty. For k=3 even two components would exceed the cap b≥4, so assume k≥4.

There are exactly two high components. Indeed, let m≥2 be their number. The previous high-contact argument at p gives k≥m(k−1)−Σ a_i, where a_i counts the high p-contacts in these components. Since Σ a_i≤b≤k, one obtains m(k−1)≤2k and hence m=2.

Any vertex in a high component lies at distance at most two from the set {p,q}. Otherwise it and a nonattachment in the other component would be at distance at least five: every path meets the separating set, and the other vertex is itself at distance at least two from it. The upstream general diameter bound and its distance-five endpoint theorem together rule out any high-high pair at distance at least five. Consequently every R_i is at distance exactly two from at least one anchor. Each vertex in R_1 is at distance exactly four from each vertex in R_2, by the same separator lower bound and the exclusion of larger high-high distances. The cap at a member of R_1 thus yields |R_2|≤k−1, and conversely.

There is some high common contact adjacent to p and q. If none existed, each high p-contact's closed neighborhood would contain at least k vertices in its own component, since p would be its only neighbor outside. Thus k≥2k−Σa_i and Σa_i≥k; symmetrically q would have at least k high component contacts. The two sets would be disjoint subsets of the b≤k total contacts, absurd.

Let x be such a common contact in component i. It has a neighbor in R_i, because its possible neighbors among the two anchors and P_i number at most |P_i|+1≤k−1, whereas x is high. That R_i vertex sees both anchors at distance two. It follows that |R_j|≤k−2 for the opposite component. Finally n=3+b+|R_1|+|R_2|≤3+k+(k−1)+(k−2)=3k.

This is a human proof. The only use of the full distance-five reduction, as opposed to the local distance-two cap, is the depth argument in the last subsection. The resulting order invokes the separately proved uniform 3k theorem.
