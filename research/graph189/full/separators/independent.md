# Independent audit of the two-cut obstruction

The argument in two-cut.md proves that a 2-connected graph satisfying the Graph 189 hypothesis, with a two-vertex cut leaving at least three components, has order at most \(3k\). Here \(k\) is the second-smallest degree. The proof uses the independently established order theorem for the final traceability step.

## Counting and equality at the anchors

Each component has a contact with both removed vertices. A nonsingleton component cannot have a single attachment vertex, since deleting that vertex would separate its remaining vertices from the other components. If the unique below-\(k\) vertex is not a singleton component, at least one anchor has a high neighbor in every component. If the low vertex is an anchor, all component contacts are high.

At one anchor \(p\), let \(A=\sum_i a_i\) be its number of component contacts. One \(p\)-contact in component \(i\) sees all \(A-a_i\) contacts of \(p\) outside it at distance exactly two. Summing gives \((t-1)A\le tk\), at either anchor. Conversely, for a high chosen \(p\)-contact, its closed neighborhood inside its component contains at least \(k-1\) vertices. Subtracting all \(a_i\) contacts of \(p\) in that component leaves at least \(k-1-a_i\) witnesses at distance two from \(p\). If no high contact there is common to both anchors, a chosen high contact misses \(q\) and improves this lower bound by one. Negative summands cause no problem, since these are lower bounds on nonnegative witness counts and the witness sets across components are disjoint. If \(pq\) is absent and one common contact exists, \(q\) is an additional disjoint witness.

For \(t\ge4\) and \(k\ge3\), the lower bound \(t(k-1)-k\) increases in \(t\) and the upper bound \(tk/(t-1)\) decreases. At \(t=4\), \(3k-4>4k/3\). For \(k=2\), each contact sees at least \(t-1\) contacts in other components, so \(t\le3\) directly. Thus \(t=3\). The resulting interval \(2k-3\le A\le\lfloor3k/2\rfloor\) gives \(k\le6\). If \(A=2k-3\), the improvement described above forces a high common contact in every component and forces the anchor edge \(pq\).

## Saturation and the cases \(k=2,\ldots,6\)

An anchor contact that sees \(k\) witnesses outside its component is adjacent to every other vertex within it. Otherwise a shortest path within that connected component yields a distance-two witness within it. For the near-saturation case at \(k=5\), the torso on the component and the two adjacent anchors remains connected after any single vertex deletion: excursions beyond it can be replaced by the edge between the anchors, or erased when they return to their starting anchor. Since the torso is induced, a distance-two witness there remains at distance two in the original graph. If the common contact has only one witness slot left, its torso distance-two layer has size at most one and cannot separate it from a farther vertex. Both anchors are already adjacent to the common contact, so this bounds all of its nonneighbors in the component by one.

- At \(k=6\), \(A=9\) and \(A-a_i\le6\) force \(a_i=3\) throughout. Every chosen \(p\)-contact is internally complete by the six external witnesses, so every remaining noncontact is within distance two of \(p\). This gives \(n\le17\).
- At \(k=5\), \(A=7\) and \(A-a_i\le5\) give profile sizes \(2,2,3\). A high common contact in either two-contact component exhausts its allowance on the other components' \(p\)-contacts. An outside \(q\)-only contact would give another witness via \(q\). The two saturated contacts thus exclude \(q\)-only contacts everywhere. Internal completeness holds in the two smaller profiles. In the three-contact profile the common contact sees four outside \(p\)-contacts, and the torso observation bounds missed internal vertices by one. The resulting count is \(n\le15\).
- At \(k=4\), \(A=6\) forces \(2,2,2\) and saturation. For \(A=5\), every component has order at least three and has a high common contact. With \(1,1,3\), a saturated common contact in one singleton profile forces all \(q\)-contacts of the other singleton profile to be \(p\)-contacts, creating a sole attachment in a nonsingleton component. With \(1,2,2\), the 1-profile needs a \(q\)-only contact. The common contacts of the two larger profiles then see three outside \(p\)-contacts and that fourth distinct witness via \(q\). All three components are saturated and the order is at most 11 in this case.
- At \(k=3\), no component is a singleton: a singleton is degree two and would be the excluded low vertex. If \(A=3\), each component's second attachment must be to \(q\), which already sees every one of the three common contacts. This contradicts \(q\)'s upper bound of four contacts. If \(A=4\), at most one component can lack a high common contact by the improved lower bound. Hence one 1-profile has a saturated common contact. Again that forces the other 1-profile to have a sole attachment, impossible.
- At \(k=2\), each anchor has exactly one contact per component. If any contact is common, saturation forces common contacts in the other two components and all three components are singletons by the attachment criterion. If no contact is common, all three \(q\)-contacts are at distance two from \(p\) along the edges within their respective components supplied by internal completeness at each \(p\)-contact.

## Low singleton

The singleton \(z\) must be adjacent to both anchors and to no other vertex. All port vertices in the high components are therefore at distance two from \(z\). Each high component has at least \(k-1\) vertices and at least two ports. If their total port count \(b\le k\), each high component has at most \(k-2\) ports, leaving a nonempty remainder. At \(k=3\) the minimum of four ports is already too many. For \(k\ge4\), the internal witness count at \(p\), applied to the \(m\) high components, gives \(m(k-1)\le k+\sum a_i\le2k\), so exactly two high components remain.

For any high pair in different components, distance at least five is excluded by the conjunction of the upstream R1 lemmas `diameter_le_five` and `five_endpoints` ([PR #2971](https://github.com/GuillaumeBAUD-coder/open-math-agents/pull/2971)). A vertex at distance greater than two from both anchors, paired with a nonport of the opposite component, would violate this exclusion. Thus each nonport is at distance two from at least one anchor, and all opposite nonport pairs are exactly at distance four. Each component's nonports therefore have cardinality at most \(k-1\), reserving a cap slot at the opposite nonport for an anchor. If both anchors had only private high contacts, the improved witness lower bound would force at least \(k\) disjoint contacts for each anchor, contradicting \(b\le k\). A high common contact has at most \(|P_i|+1\le k-1\) port and anchor neighbors, so it reaches some nonport in its own component. That nonport sees both anchors at distance two, reducing the opposite component's bound to \(k-2\). Altogether \(n\le3+k+(k-1)+(k-2)=3k\).

## Minimum cuts of size \(s\ge3\)

I also checked the companion classification in minimum-separator.md. Cardinality-minimality is essential: if some \(x\in S\) has no edge to a component, deleting \(S\setminus\{x\}\) leaves that component detached. For the high-contact anchor, the refined defect sum counts \(s-h_i\) when the chosen contact sees only \(h_i\) members of \(S\). The inequalities with \(t=s+1\) are the weakest in the needed monotonic comparison, since \(k-s+1\ge1\). The displayed coefficient bound and the integer floors are valid for \(s\ge3\).

A low singleton sees every port of every high component at distance two, since it sees every member of \(S\). Each high component needs two ports by 2-connectivity, so \(k\ge2s\); combined with \((s-2)k\le s(s-1)\), this leaves only \(s=3,k=6\). In that case each high component has at least four vertices and its at least three ports are forced by \(\kappa=3\): if at most two ports existed, deleting them would leave an internal vertex separated from the other components. The resulting nine witnesses exceed the cap.

If \(k=s+1\), the bound \(A\le s+2\) leaves total defect at most one, so one selected vertex sees all anchors. It sees at least two ports in each of the other \(s\) components at distance two, exceeding \(s+1\). If \(k=s\), the contact count forces \(t=A=s+1\), and total defect at most \(s\) guarantees a chosen vertex seeing all anchors. Its cap forces each other component to have exactly one port. Such a component is a singleton by 2-connectivity and sees all of \(S\) by cut minimality. A singleton's cap then forces the first component also to have exactly one port and hence to be a singleton. The resulting spanning complete bipartite subgraph has a spanning path by direct alternation between part sizes \(s\) and \(s+1\).
