# A mixed pair of triple-visible remotes

In the doubled-private triangle with \(|T|=2\), suppose one triple \(L\) has at most one common middle neighbor and the other \(H\) has at least one. Let \(F\) be the induced nontriple remote graph and assume \(F\) is two-connected. Under the full even-distance hypothesis, the graph has a Hamilton path for \(k\ge16\).

Write \(P,Q\) for the twins, \(B,C\) for the singleton privates and \(W\) for the \(k-4\) common middles. Use \(S_J\) for the nontriple support of \(J\), \(R_J\) for its full remote support, \(H_J\) for the closed remote neighborhood of \(R_J\), and \(a_J=|N(J)\cap W|\). Write \(m\) for the order of the full remote graph.

## A Hamilton cycle containing the low triple

The private remote distance-two allowance at \(J\) is \(a_J+1\), after counting the root, both foreign anchors and missed common middles. Every nontriple whose visibility is restricted to one anchor belongs to one of those foreign-private slots: its closed remote neighborhood has at least \(k-1\) members, since it has at most two private neighbors, and must contain a neighbor outside the visibility set of its anchor. As the anchor-visible sets within \(F\) have sizes at most \(k-4,k-5,k-5\), the number of such anchor-exclusive nontriples is at least \(2|F|-(3k-14)=2m-3k+10\). The total of the foreign-private slots is at most \(\sum_J(a_J+1)\le 2k-4\), giving \(m\le(5k-14)/2\).

Set \(R'=G[V(F)\cup\{L\}]\). Every nontriple loses at most three private neighbors and \(H\) on restriction to \(R'\), so it has degree at least \(k-4\) there. If \(L\) has at most three private neighbors, it loses at most five neighbors outside \(R'\): those privates, at most one common middle, and \(H\). If \(L\) has four private neighbors, then all three anchors, all its nonneighbors in \(F\), and all its missed common middles are at distance two. For the last assertion, any common middle with no private neighbor must touch both triples to attain degree \(k\). Hence \(d_F(L)\ge |F|-1-|N(L)\cap W|\ge 2k-7\). These bounds give \(\delta(R')\ge k-5\). Also \(L\) has at least two neighbors in \(F\), so \(R'\) is two-connected. Its order is at most \((5k-16)/2\).

Suppose \(R'\) contains an independent set \(X\) of \(k-4\) vertices. Its members share pairwise common \(R'\)-neighbors, since \(2(k-5)>(3k-8)/2\ge |R'\setminus X|\). Thus a nontriple \(x\in X\) sees the other \(k-5\) members at distance two. For any private \(J\) supporting \(x\), it also sees all \(a_J\) common neighbors of \(J\), and at least two more vertices outside \(W\) and \(R'\): two anchors if \(x\) is visible to both, or its anchor and a foreign private through a remote neighbor outside its anchor visibility set. Thus \(a_J\le3\). Both twins have \(a_J\ge4\), since \(|R_J|\le k-5\) and \(k\le |H_J|\le |R_J|+a_J+1\). Therefore only the two singleton privates can support a nontriple in \(X\). For each such \(J\), choose some \(x\in S_J\cap X\). Its at least \(k-4\) neighbors in \(R'\) lie in \(H_J\setminus X\), so
\[
 |S_J\cap X|\le |H_J|-(k-4)\le a_J+2\le5
\]
using \(|R_J|\le k-3\). Hence \(|X|\le 5+5+1=11<k-4\). This proves \(\alpha(R')\le k-5\).

The order condition \(k-5\ge(\lfloor(5k-16)/2\rfloor+2)/3\) holds for \(k\ge17\). For \(k=16\) we save one incidence in the order bound. If every common middle were paired, both singleton privates would pair. Their partner must be the same twin by the exclusion of complementary pairs. Thus the other twin and both singleton privates would have disjoint common neighborhoods of sizes at least \(5,4,4\), using the strengthened bounds proved in the next section. This is impossible with \(|W|=12\). At least one common middle contributes fewer than two private incidences, so \(2m\le5k-15\) and \(|R'|\le31\). Thus \(\delta(R')\ge (|R'|+2)/3\) also at \(k=16\). Nash-Williams gives a Hamilton cycle of \(R'\) in either case.

## Outer assembly with the high triple

We first strengthen the common-neighbor bounds using the full even-distance cap. For each private \(J\),
\[
 k\le |H_J|\le |S_J|+a_J+3.
\]
If equality holds between the endpoints, both triples lie in \(R_J\), and the closed remote neighborhood of each \(J\)-solo equals \(H_J\). All \(a_J+1\) gates in \(H_J\setminus R_J\), the root, the foreign anchors and the missed common middles exhaust the fixed even-distance allowance at \(J\). Every remote outside \(H_J\) is nontriple, so it has distance at most four from \(J\) by going through the anchor of \(J\), a common middle, and a private that supports the remote. All privates have positive \(a\), since already \(|R_J|\le k-3\) implies \(a_J\ge2\). The distance from \(J\) to the outsider is therefore three, and the penultimate vertex is at distance two from \(J\). It must be a gate, since none of the fixed nonremote witnesses can neighbor a nontriple. Consequently any \(J\)-solo sees all \(m-k\) such outsiders at distance two, as it is adjacent to every gate. It also sees the \(a_J\) common neighbors of \(J\), its anchor, and a foreign private through a remote neighbor outside its anchor visibility set. The total is \(m-k+a_J+2>k\) for \(a_J\ge2\).

Since the anchor-visible sets in \(F\) have sizes at most \(k-4,k-5,k-5\), the nontriple support of a twin has size at most \(k-7\) (subtract the other twin's three solos), and that of a singleton is at most \(k-5\). Consequently the strict inequality \(k<|S_J|+a_J+3\) gives \(a_P,a_Q\ge5\) and \(a_B,a_C\ge3\). A paired private has nontriple support at most \(k-6\), as the two supports in a pair have union at most \(k-3\), and the opposite private has three solos. Therefore every paired private has \(a_J\ge4\).

Reserve a neighbor \(r\in W\) of \(H\), and assign disjoint pairs of neighbors in \(W\setminus\{r\}\) to \(Q,B,C\) by Hall's theorem. Before excluding \(r\), each individual neighborhood has at least three members. Each union of two has at least five, since the two singleton neighborhoods are disjoint and the twin has at least five on its own. The union of all three has at least seven if the two singleton degrees sum to at least seven. Otherwise both singleton degrees are three and neither singleton pairs, so their neighborhoods are disjoint from one another and from those of \(Q\), again giving at least seven. After excluding \(r\), these bounds meet the demand of two common neighbors per private.

Start with \(H,r\). Every common middle misses at most two other common middles, because it has at most three anchors, two privates, and two triples outside \(W\). The three assigned blocks \(w,J,w'\) can be traversed in succession without any additional common middles. To verify this, consider two blocks with endpoints \(a,a'\) and \(b,b'\), and any preceding common middle \(u\). If there is a cross-edge \(ab\), traverse the blocks in one of the two orders using this edge whenever \(u\) is adjacent to \(a'\) or \(b'\). Otherwise \(u\) misses \(a',b'\) and is adjacent to \(a,b\). Any additional cross-edge now gives the required traversal, since at least one of its two opposite endpoints is adjacent to \(u\); if \(ab\) were the unique cross-edge, \(a'\) and \(b'\) would already miss both endpoints in the opposite block and could not miss \(u\). Thus two blocks can be entered consecutively whenever any cross-edge exists. Among three blocks, at most one pair has no cross-edge, since its four endpoints each miss exactly the two endpoints of the opposite block. If such a pair exists, start with one of its blocks, whose endpoints are adjacent to \(r\), and then the other two have a cross-edge. Otherwise start with any block having an endpoint adjacent to \(r\), which exists because \(r\) misses at most two out of six. Apply the two-block argument after the first block.

The remaining \(|W|-7=k-11\ge5\) common middles together with the current endpoint induce a graph on at least six vertices of minimum degree at least its order minus three. Dirac gives a Hamilton cycle in this graph. Start that cycle at the current endpoint and follow it as a path through the previously unvisited vertices.

From the final common middle append the anchor of \(B\), the root, the anchor of \(C\), the shared twin anchor, and \(P\). Then enter a Hamilton cycle of \(R'\) at a \(P\)-solo and follow the entire cycle as a path. This covers the graph, including both triples.


## When both triples have no common middle neighbors

Instead keep both triples in the augmented remote graph \(R\). Every nontriple has degree at least \(k-3\) in \(R\). A triple with at most three private neighbors has the same bound since it has no common middle neighbors. A triple with four private neighbors has remote degree at least \(|F|-1\) by the earlier full-private argument. Thus \(\delta(R)\ge k-3\), and each triple has at least \(k-4\) neighbors in \(F\). The graph \(R\) is two-connected and has order \(m\le(5k-14)/2\).

For an independent set \(X\) of size \(k-2\), every two members share a remote neighbor since \(2(k-3)>(3k-10)/2\ge |R\setminus X|\). A nontriple in \(X\) would see the other \(k-3\) members, at least two common middles of one of its supporting privates, and at least two anchor or foreign-private witnesses, contradicting the distance-two cap. There is a nontriple in \(X\) since \(k\ge16\). Thus \(\alpha(R)\le k-3\), and Nash-Williams gives a Hamilton cycle in \(R\).

To assemble the outer path, use the same Hall argument without reserving or excluding \(r\). Start directly with a private block, choosing one of the two incompatible blocks if a block pair has no cross-edge. Then use the two-block argument above, without connectors. The tail has one more available common middle and the same Dirac argument applies. Append the anchors, root, twin, and a spanning path along the Hamilton cycle of \(R\) as before.
