# One extra double middle, one triple remote, and a two-connected nontriple layer

Consider the triangular anchor template with nonuniversal masks \(1,2,3,4\) and universal common middles \(W\). Call the three anchors \(A,B,C\), their true private middles \(P,Q,J\), and the double middle adjoining \(A,B\) by \(D\). The root has degree three and eccentricity at most three. Suppose the graph has order greater than \(3k\), every vertex except the root has degree at least \(k\), and each vertex has at most \(k\) other vertices at positive even distance. There is exactly one triple-visible remote \(t\). Let \(F\) be the induced nontriple remote graph, \(m=|F|\), and assume \(F\) is two-connected. Then the graph has a Hamilton path for \(k\ge9\).

Each nontriple remote is supported by one or more vertices in \(\{P,Q,J,D\}\). A nontriple supported by \(D\) cannot also touch \(J\), so every nontriple has at most three supporting middles. For a supporting middle \(I\), write \(S_I,R_I,H_I\) for its nontriple support, full remote support, and closed remote neighborhood of the full support, respectively. Write \(a_I=|W\cap N(I)|\). We have \(|W|=k-4\) and \(m\ge2k-4\). Write \(V_A,V_B,V_C\) for the sets of remotes visible to the three anchors.

## Common and remote incidence bounds

The anchors' remote visibility sizes are bounded above by \(k-2,k-2,k-3\): \(A,B\) have the two other true privates as fixed distance-two witnesses, and \(C\) also sees \(D\). Deleting \(t\) gives nontriple visibility caps \(k-3,k-3,k-4\). Since all three visibility sets contain \(t\) and the remote order is at least \(2k-3\), the anchor-exclusive classes have sizes at least \(3,3,2\). These exclusive remotes are solo neighbors of their corresponding true private. In particular
\[
 |R_P|,|R_Q|\le k-2,\qquad |R_J|\le k-3,\qquad |R_D|\le k-5,
\]
where the last inequality subtracts \(A\)'s at least three exclusive remotes from \(V_A\).

Each common middle has one or two neighbors in \(\{P,Q,J,D\}\). At least one is required by degree \(k\), since its other potential neighbors are three anchors, the \(k-5\) other common middles, and \(t\). If it touched exactly three support middles then their supports would cover all nontriple remotes outside the visibility set of a suitable anchor: the omitted support is contained in \(V_A\) for \(P\) or \(D\), in \(V_B\) for \(Q\), and in \(V_C\) for \(J\). Thus at least \(k-1\) nontriples would be at distance two alongside the root and the missed supporting middle. Touching all four puts all nontriples at distance two. Also, any two supporting middles \(I,I'\) paired at a common middle have \(|S_I\cup S_{I'}|\le k-3\), by counting the root and the two missed supporting middles. Complementary pairs are therefore impossible because \(m\ge2k-4>2(k-3)\). Finally each common middle misses at most one other common middle, by its degree and the upper bound on its neighbors outside \(W\).

At each true private \(I\) the root, two other anchors and missed common middles are at distance two, so its remote distance-two allowance is \(a_I+1\). At \(D\) the allowance is \(a_D+2\), since only the third anchor is at distance two. The total allowance is at most \(2|W|+5=2k-3\). Each anchor-exclusive nontriple \(x\) has one supporting middle and its closed remote neighborhood has size at least \(k\), larger than that anchor's visibility set. Thus a remote neighbor outside this visibility set puts \(x\) at distance two from a foreign supporting middle. Call that ordered pair of remote and supporting middle a foreign incidence. For \(U\) anchor-exclusive remotes, counting one such incidence apiece gives
\[
 2m-3k+10\le U\le 2k-3,\qquad m\le(5k-13)/2.
\]
Here the lower bound comes from the sum of the three nontriple visibility caps, because each nontriple is visible to either one or two anchors.

## Lower bounds on the true private common degrees

A true private's solo closed remote neighborhood has size at least \(k\) and lies in \(H_I\), while \(|H_I|\le |R_I|+a_I+1\). This initially gives \(a_P,a_Q\ge1\) and \(a_J\ge2\). Also \(a_D>0\): if \(S_D\ne\varnothing\), the closed neighborhood of any nontriple supporter has size at least \(k-2\), so \(|H_D|\le k-5+a_D+2\) implies \(a_D\ge1\). If \(S_D=\varnothing\) then \(D\) has at most one remote neighbor and degree forces \(a_D\ge k-6\).

When \(|S_I|+a_I+2=k\) at a true private, \(|H_I|=k\) and \(t\in R_I\). Then all \(a_I+1\) remote distance-two slots are occupied by \(H_I\setminus R_I\), and every solo is adjacent to all of these gates. Each nontriple outsider of \(H_I\) is at distance at most four from \(I\) along a route through its anchor, a common middle, and an outsider's supporting middle, using positivity of the common degrees. It must be at distance three as all even-distance slots are occupied. The penultimate vertex must be a gate, since every counted nonremote witness has no nontriple neighbors. Thus each solo sees all \((m+1)-k\) outsiders at distance two. It also sees \(a_I\) common middles, one anchor, and a foreign supporting middle through a remote neighbor outside its anchor visibility. This rules out saturation whenever \(a_I\ge2\), because \((m+1)-k+a_I+2>k\).

For \(P,Q\) there is a further exclusion at \(a_I=1\). If the true private is nonadjacent to \(D\) then \(D\) occupies an additional distance-two slot through their anchor. In this case \(|H_I|\le |R_I|+a_I<k\). If the true private is adjacent to \(D\), its solos see \(D\) at distance two in addition to the prior fixed witnesses: the foreign supporting middle through a neighbor outside its anchor visibility cannot be \(D\), as \(D\) supports only remotes visible to both \(A,B\). Now saturation gives one extra witness, so it is again impossible. We obtain
\[
 a_P,a_Q\ge2,\qquad a_J\ge3 .
\]
If \(P,Q\) are paired, their supports each have size at most \(k-6\), since the other contains three solos; the initial lower bound \(a\ge4\) strengthens to \(a\ge5\) by the saturation exclusion. If \(P,J\) or \(Q,J\) are paired, the same reasoning gives \(a_J\ge5\), using the opposite true private's three solos. Thus any two true private common neighborhoods that intersect have union of size at least five, and low common neighborhoods of \(P,Q\) cannot intersect.

## Hamiltonicity of the nontriple layer

We have \(\delta(F)\ge k-4\). Suppose \(X\) is an independent set of \(k-3\) vertices in \(F\). Each pair shares a neighbor in \(F\), since \(2(k-4)>(3k-7)/2\ge |F\setminus X|\) for \(k\ge10\). For \(k=9\), the order bound is integral: \(|F|\le16\) implies \(|F\setminus X|\le10=2(k-4)\). If two such vertices have no common neighbor in \(F\), both must have degree five in \(F\). They must then both have three supports and are at distance two through a common supporting middle. Thus the distance-two conclusion holds for all \(k\ge9\). Each \(x\in X\) sees its \(k-4\) companions at distance two, plus two distinct witnesses outside \(F\cup W\): two anchors if it is visible to two, or its own anchor and a foreign supporting middle through an outside-visibility remote neighbor if it is anchor-exclusive. For every supporting middle \(I\) of \(x\), it also sees all \(a_I\) common neighbors of \(I\). Thus \(a_I\le2\), and no member of \(X\) is supported by \(J\). No member touches both \(P,Q\): if both these middles have common degree two their neighborhoods are disjoint, giving at least four common witnesses plus the two anchors. Hence each member of \(X\) has at most two supports and at least \(k-3\) neighbors in \(F\).

If \(x\in X\cap S_I\), all its neighbors in \(F\) lie in \(H_I\setminus X\), giving \(|X\cap S_I|\le |H_I|-(k-3)\). The bound is at most four for \(P,Q\) and at most two for \(D\). A low true private \(I=P,Q\) with a solo \(x\) in \(X\) in fact satisfies \(|X\cap S_I|\le2\). Otherwise \(|H_I|\le k+1\) would be partitioned into three members of \(X\cap S_I\) and at least \(k-2\) distinct \(F\)-neighbors of \(x\), excluding \(t\). Equality requires \(|R_I|=k-2\), which forces \(t\in R_I\), since the entire anchor visibility set has at most \(k-2\) vertices and contains \(t\). A low true private without a solo in \(X\) contributes at most two as well, because all of its \(X\) members also belong to \(S_D\). If some member has two supports, it is counted in both \(S_D\) and a true support, giving \(|X|\le2+2+2-1=5\). If none has two supports, every member of \(X\cap S_D\) has at least \(k-2\) neighbors in \(F\), so the \(D\)-contribution drops to one, again giving \(|X|\le5\). This contradicts \(k-3\ge6\), so \(\alpha(F)\le k-4\).

For \(k\ge14\), Nash-Williams' theorem applies, since \(k-4\ge(\lfloor(5k-13)/2\rfloor+2)/3\). For \(k=13\), it suffices to exclude \(m=26\). In that case \(U\ge23\) and the allowance is at most 23, so each anchor-exclusive has exactly one foreign incidence and there are no other incidences. In particular exactly three nontriple vertices are nonexclusive. Any \(y\in S_D\) cannot neighbor an \(A\)- or \(B\)-exclusive, which would create an extra same-anchor incidence at \(D\). It cannot neighbor a \(C\)-exclusive, which would create an incidence for the nonexclusive \(y\) at \(J\). Thus \(y\) cannot have enough neighbors in \(F\) and \(S_D=\varnothing\). The middle \(D\) cannot neighbor \(P\) or \(Q\), because this makes their solos extra same-anchor incidences at \(D\). Nor can \(D\) neighbor \(J\): its solos would have an incidence at \(D\) in addition to their necessary foreign incidence through a nontriple remote neighbor, since \(D\) has no nontriple support. Then the degree of \(D\) is at most two anchors, nine common middles and \(t\), totaling 12, contradiction.

For \(9\le k\le12\), apply the degree-sum Hamiltonicity criterion for a two-connected graph, \(\sigma_3\ge\max\{m+2,3\alpha\}\). The bound \(\alpha(F)\le k-4\) already takes care of \(3\alpha\). The inequality \(3(k-4)\ge m+2\) holds except in the profiles of the table. In the table, \(e=5k-13-2m\) is an upper bound on the number of additional remote incidences beyond one foreign incidence for each anchor-exclusive remote, and \(d\le3k-10-m\) bounds the nonexclusive remotes.

| \(k\) | \(m\) needing a stronger bound | \(e\) | Bound on \(d\) | Required \(\sigma_3\) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 9 | 14 | 4 | 3 | 16 |
| 9 | 15 | 2 | 2 | 17 |
| 9 | 16 | 0 | 1 | 18 |
| 10 | 17 | 3 | 3 | 19 |
| 10 | 18 | 1 | 2 | 20 |
| 11 | 20 | 2 | 3 | 22 |
| 11 | 21 | 0 | 2 | 23 |
| 12 | 23 | 1 | 3 | 25 |

Every \(y\in S_D\) has at least \(k-4\) neighbors in \(F\), at least \(k-3-d\) of which are anchor-exclusive. Each \(A\)- or \(B\)-exclusive neighbor consumes an additional incidence at \(D\), so there are at most \(e\) such neighbors. Across all rows \(k-3-d>e\) except the row \(k=9,m=14\). Thus in the other rows each \(D\)-supporter neighbors a \(C\)-exclusive, and each such \(y\) then contributes an additional incidence at \(J\). In particular \(|S_D|\le e\). When \(e=0\), no nontriple has three supports, and \(\sigma_3\ge3(k-3)\), meeting the table. When \(e=1\), at most one has three supports, so \(\sigma_3\ge(k-4)+2(k-3)\), again sufficient. When \(k=11,m=20\), at most two have three supports and \(\sigma_3\ge2(k-4)+(k-3)=22\).

For \(k=9,m=15\), any three-supported \(y\) would touch a \(C\)-exclusive \(x\) as just proved. A three-supported \(y\) has \(P,Q,D\) as its supports, so that \(x\) has at least two extra foreign incidences beyond its first, in addition to the extra incidence of \(y\) at \(J\). This exceeds \(e=2\), so all nontriple degrees are at least \(k-3\). For \(k=10,m=17\), an independent triple of vertices of degree \(k-4\) would consist of three fully supported vertices \(y\in S_D\). Each consumes an extra incidence at \(J\), exhausting the \(e=3\) extras, and the two additional foreign incidences at a \(C\)-exclusive neighbor contradict this. Thus \(\sigma_3\ge19\). Finally for \(k=9,m=14\), such an independent triple would exhaust the at most three nonexclusive vertices. Then all \(k-4=5\) neighbors in \(F\) of each member are exclusive. At most \(e=4\) are \(A\)- or \(B\)-exclusive, so each has a \(C\)-exclusive neighbor. Again there are three additional incidences at \(J\) and at least two more via a \(C\)-exclusive neighbor, exceeding \(e=4\). Thus \(\sigma_3\ge16\). This checks every exceptional profile.

## Assembly

First suppose \(t\) has a common middle neighbor \(r\). We will choose one of \(P,Q\) as the endpoint of the outer path, and insert the other one and \(J\) as disjoint blocks through pairs of their common neighbors. For the reserved \(r\), at most one of \(P,Q\) both has common degree two and neighbors \(r\), since a \(P,Q\) pair has both common degrees at least five. Choose that private as the endpoint if it exists. The other true private and \(J\) each have at least two available common neighbors after excluding \(r\), and their union has at least four: their original union has at least five by the lower bounds if disjoint and the pairing bound above otherwise. Hall's theorem therefore gives the two disjoint reserved pairs of common neighbors.

Start \(t,r\), then append the two blocks, orienting each so that the first common middle is adjacent to the preceding endpoint. Every common middle misses at most one, so this is always possible. For \(k\ge12\), the unused \(|W|-5\ge3\) common middles together with the current endpoint induce a graph with order at least four and minimum degree at least order minus two, so use Dirac to traverse them from the current endpoint as a path. At \(k=11\) there are two unused common middles. If adjacent, orient their pair to append it from the current endpoint. Otherwise both have \(t\) as a neighbor: any common middle missing another common middle needs all possible remaining neighbors, including \(t\), to reach degree \(k\). Exchange one unused middle with the reserved \(r\). The now-unused two are adjacent, since one's unique nonneighbor is the new reserved middle, and the two block orientations still exist. At \(k=10\) there is one unused middle \(w\). If the current endpoint misses it, again \(w\) neighbors \(t\). Replace \(r\) with \(w\) and keep the same ordering and orientation of the two blocks; the new \(r\) misses only the final block endpoint and so can enter the first block. The final block endpoint then sees the old \(r\), now the unused middle. At \(k=9\) all common middles are used by \(r\) and the two blocks, so no tail is needed. If the chosen private endpoint is \(Q\), append \(C,z,A,D,B,Q\), and if it is \(P\), append \(C,z,B,D,A,P\). Enter the Hamilton cycle of \(F\) at a solo of the chosen private and follow it as a path.

If \(t\) has no common middle neighbors, instead use the full remote graph \(R=F+t\). If \(t\) has at most three supporting neighbors then it has remote degree at least \(k-3\). If it has four, it sees all missed nontriples, all three anchors, and all common middles at distance two, since each common middle has a supporting neighbor. Hence it has at least \(m-1\) neighbors in \(F\). Thus \(\delta(R)\ge k-3\) and \(R\) is two-connected. An independent set of \(k-2\) has all pairs sharing a remote neighbor by the same order/degree bound. Each nontriple in it sees \(k-3\) companions and at least two anchor/foreign-support witnesses, so every support must have \(a\le1\). Only \(D\) is possible; its members then have at least \(k-3\) neighbors in \(R\), and the closed support bound gives at most one independent member from \(S_D\). Adding at most \(t\) still leaves too few. Hence \(\alpha(R)\le k-3\). The inequality \(k-3\ge(|R|+2)/3\) holds for \(k\ge10\), because \(|R|\le\lfloor(5k-11)/2\rfloor\), and also for \(k=9\) if \(|R|\le16\). In those cases use Nash-Williams. At \(k=9,\ |R|=17\), we have \(m=16\), where the table argument gives \(S_D=\varnothing\). Thus ordinary nontriple vertices lose at most two private neighbors on restriction to \(R\), and \(\sigma_3(R)\ge6+7+7=20\) meets the degree-sum criterion. In every case \(R\) has a Hamilton cycle. Start the same outer path directly with the first insertion block, reserving no \(r\), and enter the cycle of \(R\) instead. The common middles form a clique in this no-\(t\)-neighbor case: each common middle has at most five neighbors outside \(W\), and so requires all other common middles to reach degree \(k\).
