# Two-component separators and the parity of an adjacent pair

Paper proof by gpt-6.2-astra. Let G be a finite simple connected graph of second-smallest degree k in which each vertex sees at most k other vertices at positive even distance. A high vertex has degree at least k. We use the upstream diameter bound ≤5 and the theorem that any distance-five pair includes a below-k vertex (OMA PR 2971). Hence any pair of high vertices has distance at most four. Easwar–Johari already prove the cut-vertex case in Theorem B(4) of https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.11719. We work with a 2-connected G and two separating vertices p,q whose deletion leaves precisely two components. A port touches at least one of p,q, and a common port touches both. Assume that each component has a high nonport.

**Opposite nonports.** Two high nonports in opposite components have distance exactly four, as every path must visit a separator vertex. The middle vertex of any such shortest path is p or q. For a high nonport x, let T(x) be the nonempty set of separator vertices at distance two from x. Then T(x) and T(y) intersect whenever x,y are high nonports on opposite sides. Each component contains at most k−2 high nonports. Indeed, a high nonport y in the opposite component sees all these vertices at distance four and at least one anchor at distance two. If k−1 such vertices existed, every even-distance slot would be used outside the component of y. Thus y is adjacent to every other vertex of its component: otherwise a shortest internal path from y to a missed vertex has a distance-two witness at its second step. Every component has a p-contact and a q-contact by 2-connectivity, hence both p and q are at distance two from y, a contradiction.

**Adjacent separators with a common-port-free side.** Suppose pq is an edge and one component D contains no common port. Then n≤3k. In particular the order-through-3k theorem of the earlier fork research gives traceability.

For the proof, let O and E count the vertices equidistant from p and q at odd and even distances, respectively. At every other vertex precisely one of its two distances is even, because those distances differ by one. Summing the inclusive even-distance counts at p and q gives n+E−O≤2k+2. Let c be the number of common ports. A high vertex has distance at most two from at least one anchor, as was established for high nonports above. Consequently O≤c+1. If there is an extra odd tie, it is the unique low nonport z at distance three from each anchor. Indeed an opposite high nonport is at least two more edges away, while the maximum diameter is five. In this case E≥1. Otherwise every high nonport has singleton T and the cross-intersection property forces all such singletons on both sides to be the same anchor. The first steps on shortest paths from z to p and to q, however, reach high nonports in its component whose T-sets contain p and q, respectively. Thus O−E≤c.

Both types of private port occur in D. Any p-contact there sees q and all c common ports at distance two, hence c≤k−1. If c≤k−2 the last inequality gives n≤3k. If c=k−1, each p-contact in D is saturated on q and the opposite common ports. No opposite p-only port is possible, and every p-contact in D is adjacent to its whole component. The corresponding assertions hold for each q-contact in D. Thus in the other component C all ports are common. Every high nonport of C sees both anchors at distance two, because its nearest port is common, and every high nonport of D sees a port of each anchor by the preceding internal adjacency. There is a high nonport on each side, yielding E≥2. As O≤c+1, we obtain n≤3k also in this case.

**Separators with common ports on both sides, when every vertex is high.** The graph is traceable, regardless of whether pq is an edge. An opposite common port sees every port at distance two, giving at most k ports in each side. The first lemma bounds the remaining vertices by k−2. Thus each component has order at most 2k−2.

Write C,D for the two components. Use ordinary path closure: adding a missing edge whose endpoint degrees sum to at least the graph order minus one preserves traceability. On C together with p,q and a new leaf adjacent only to q, complete C by this rule: every C vertex retains its degree at least k and the closure threshold is |C|+2≤2k. In the completed graph there is a spanning path from the leaf via q, through C, to p. Indeed the two anchors have distinct usable contacts in C: failure would make the only attachment a cut vertex, since C has a nonport. Thus in the original graph on C,p,q there is a spanning path with endpoint q, by removing the leaf after reversing closure. The other endpoint need not be p.

Take D together with q and a new q-leaf. The threshold is |D|+1 and each D vertex has degree at least k−1, since only p was lost. If |D|≤2k−3 then closure completes D. If |D|=2k−2, all D vertices missing p have degree at least k and can join every vertex of D. If there are at least k−1 of them, choose k−1 now-universal vertices and alternate with the k−1 others, starting at any q-contact. Otherwise exactly k vertices are p-contacts and these exhaust all D ports. There are k−2 universal nonports. There is an original edge between two ports: if all k ports were independent, a common port would see the other k−1 via p plus at least two opposite ports via p or q, exceeding k. (The opposite component contains a nonport and thus at least two attachments by 2-connectivity.) Order the ports starting at any q-contact with the ends of this one edge consecutive, interleaving the k−2 universal nonports at the other gaps. This gives a spanning path of the augmented graph from the leaf through q. Reversing closure and removing the leaf yields a spanning D,q path with endpoint q. Joining the two constructed paths at q proves traceability.

In particular, when the minimum degree equals k, an adjacent two-vertex separator with nonports on both sides always implies traceability. For arbitrary minimum degree the common-port-free conclusion only needs the stated high nonport assumption. Both claims concern two-component separators, beyond the previous three-component and low-anchor fork results. No full Lean proof is claimed.

## Removing the equal-minimum-degree restriction

**Theorem.** Suppose a two-vertex separator has two components, both containing a high nonport. If the separating vertices are adjacent, or both sides have a common port, the graph is traceable.

The case of adjacent separators with a common-port-free side was proved by the order bound above. Suppose both sides have a common port. The all-high gluing argument works unchanged when only a separator vertex has degree below k. We may thus suppose the unique below-k vertex z belongs to C, with D all high. Write P for the C ports. Their number is at most k and the high nonports U number at most k−2. The order |C| is at most 2k−1 and |D|≤2k−2. The previous one-anchor argument gives a spanning path in D with either selected anchor q adjoined, starting at q.

When |C|≤2k−2, adjoin the two anchors and a q-leaf and apply the previous path closure only to the high vertices of C, making them a clique. We may exchange p and q freely before choosing the leaf. For adjacent anchors, if z is a port choose p adjacent to z, and start leaf,q,p,z,a with a high internal neighbor of z. Complete through the high clique. If z is a nonport it has two high internal neighbors. Start leaf,q,p,a with a high p-contact. Order the high clique starting at a and insert z between two of its neighbors: this pair can start at a when a is one of its ends.

For nonadjacent anchors, if z is a common port, start leaf,q,z,p,a with a high contact of p, exchanging anchors as needed. Another attachment besides z exists by 2-connectivity, since C has a nonport. If z is a private port, label its anchor p and choose a common (high) port c. When z has an internal neighbor a different from c, start leaf,q,c,p,z,a and finish in the high clique. If it has no such neighbor, then its only internal neighbor is c. Some other port b besides c,z must exist, since otherwise c would cut off C minus c,z (this set contains the given high nonport). If b touches p start leaf,q,c,z,p,b. If it touches q start instead with a p-leaf,p,z,c,q,b. In both cases the remaining high clique can follow freely.

Finally suppose z is a nonport and pq is absent. Fix a common port c and a different port b, and exchange labels so b touches q. If z has an internal neighbor a outside {b,c}, start with a p-leaf,p,c,q,b, traverse all remaining high vertices ending at a, and finish at z. Such c,b,a exist whenever z has at least three neighbors. They also exist when there are at least three ports: with a fixed c there are at least two choices for b, and the two-element sets {c,b} cannot all contain the two or more neighbors of z. In the last possibility z has precisely two neighbors, and the only ports b,c are those neighbors. Removing b and c separates z, the other high nonport(s) in C, and the opposite side into at least three components. The previous fork theorem for such a two-cut gives n≤3k and traceability (alternatively use the previous degree-two theorem). Thus in all smaller-component cases, unless the whole graph is already settled, traceability in the closure produces a spanning path of C,p,q rooted at the chosen anchor. Glue it to the path on D and that anchor.

It remains to handle |C|=2k−1. Equality forces k ports P, k−2 high nonports U, and the additional low nonport z. Every common port in D sees all P at distance two and hence is adjacent to all other D vertices. Thus each D nonport has distance two to both anchors. It sees U at distance four, so its entire even-distance allowance is used on U and the anchors. It too is universal within D. Moreover z cannot have distance two to either anchor, since it would then lie at distance four from the chosen D nonport. Consequently every neighbor of z lies in U. There are at most k−1 ports in D: if there were k, an opposite common port in C would be universal within C, impossible since z has no port neighbor. Thus |D|≤2k−3. Every nonedge in D joins two private ports, as nonports and common ports are universal there. Each private port has degree at least k−1 within D. Consequently every nonadjacent degree sum in D is at least 2k−2≥|D|+1. It follows that D is Hamilton-connected: given two prescribed endpoints, add a vertex adjacent only to them, complete D by ordinary cycle closure in the augmented graph, and remove the new vertex from the resulting original Hamilton cycle.

There is an edge e joining two C ports, since otherwise a common port would see the other k−1 members of P at distance two through the appropriate anchors and at least two D ports through those same anchors. There are at least three C ports, since |U|=k−2≥1. Choose a common port c and another port b such that f={b,c} differs from e. Construct the auxiliary graph on C and a marker m joined to all common ports, and to b if it is private. Every two distinct neighbors of m can be assigned to different anchors: they include at least one common port, since at most one is private. Every high port has auxiliary degree at least k−1, since a common port loses two anchor edges but gains the marker edge and a private port loses just one. Every U vertex retains degree at least k. The auxiliary order is 2k, so ordinary path closure may join every U vertex to every other high C vertex. The original edges incident to z and m are retained without modifications.

The distinct pairs e and f form a linear forest on P (they may share a vertex). Order P so that both pairs occur consecutively. Start a path at z and one of its U-neighbors, then enter the first port. Use e directly, insert m at the gap f, and fill the remaining k−3 gaps with the remaining k−3 members of U. This path spans the closure, proving that the original auxiliary graph is traceable. On such an original path, replace an internal m by p, a spanning D path between original distinct contacts, and q, orienting the anchors according to the two marker neighbors. If m is an endpoint, orient the two anchors to meet the one adjoining marker neighbor and use the same replacement. Distinct anchor contacts in D are available by 2-connectivity, since D contains a nonport. This gives a spanning path of G and completes the proof.

## Common-port-free adjacent separators without any depth assumption

**Theorem.** If p,q are adjacent and one of the two components of G−{p,q} has no common port, G is traceable regardless of where its nonports lie. When both components contain a high vertex, n≤3k. Otherwise the component with no high vertex is a singleton of degree two, already covered by the earlier minimum-degree-two theorem.

Suppose both sides contain a high vertex. Each side therefore has order at least k−1. The second-distance layer of a vertex with eccentricity at least three has at least two members: its deletion separates that vertex from vertices at distance three or more.

No high vertex x can be an odd tie beyond the common ports. Indeed, if it were, its distance to both p and q would be three or more. Every high vertex of the opposite component must then be exactly four away, since high pairs have distance at most four. As x also has at least two distance-two vertices, the opposite side has at most k−2 high vertices. Its order is at least k−1, so it has order exactly k−1 including one low vertex z. Every high vertex on that side must be complete to its component and both anchors to achieve degree k. If z is a port, its distance to x is four just like the other ports, contradicting the cap at x. Thus z is a nonport and the opposite component is a clique with all its k−2 high vertices common. Now z has distance five from x and every edge between its first two distance layers is present. This is excluded by the diameter-five complete-incidence lemma; here is a direct proof in this special case.

The first two layers from z comprise its k−2 high neighbors A and the two anchors, respectively. Inside the other component let P be all ports, R the vertices at distance two from the anchor set, and F those at distance three. These exhaust the component since z has eccentricity at most five, and F contains x. Put c=|P|, r=|R|, f=|F|. The cap at z gives r≤k−2. A vertex of F has degree at least k, drawn entirely from R and F, so f+r≥k+1 and f≥3. Each A vertex sees every P vertex at distance two and every F vertex at distance four. Thus c+f≤k. Every far vertex misses at most j=r+f−1−k vertices among R and the rest of F. A port with more than j neighbors in R would therefore see every F vertex at distance two, and also all of A at distance two, exceeding k because k−2+f>k. Each port consequently has at most j neighbors in R, making its degree at most 2+c−1+j≤r≤k−2. This contradicts that all ports are high.

If a low odd tie beyond the ports exists, it lies at distance three from each anchor and its own component has high nonports (the first steps on its geodesics to the anchors). If the opposite component had no high nonport, it would consist entirely of high ports and have size at least k−1. The low tie would then see all these ports at distance four plus its at least two distance-two witnesses, a contradiction. Hence both sides have high nonports, and the cross-intersection argument proved above offsets this odd tie with an even tie. Thus in all cases O−E≤c, where c is the number of common ports.

The bound c≤k−1 and its saturation characterization from the earlier proof do not require nonports on either side. If c≤k−2 the edge sum gives n≤3k. At c=k−1, suppose first there is an extra low odd tie. Then both sides have high nonports and the earlier bound E≥2 applies. If instead there is no such tie, it suffices to find a single even tie. Every nonport in the common-free side is adjacent to a port of each anchor by saturation, hence is such a tie. In the other side all ports are common, so if it has a nonport, choose one at minimum distance from the anchor set: it has distance exactly two from both anchors. Finally if neither side has a nonport, the all-common side has size k−1 and an element of it sees the entire opposite side at distance two, so n≤2k+1. This finishes the order bound.
