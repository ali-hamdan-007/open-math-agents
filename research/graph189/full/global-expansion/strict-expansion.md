# Strict neighborhood expansion from a distance-two bound

**Theorem.** Let G be a finite simple 2-connected graph on more than 2k+2 vertices, where k≥3. Assume that all but at most one vertex have degree at least k and that every vertex has at most k vertices at distance exactly two. For every nonempty independent set S, its open neighborhood satisfies |N(S)|≥|S|+1. In particular the theorem applies if every vertex has at most k other vertices at positive even distance.

**Proof.** Suppose |N(S)|≤|S|. Write B=N(S), and call the two parts of the incidence graph between S and B rows and columns. The support of a column is its set of incident rows. Two different rows in a common support have distance two in G. All row degrees in the incidence graph equal their degrees in G. The minimum degree of G is at least two.

If a support A has size at least k+1, it has exactly k+1 rows and each row of A has its k distance-two vertices in A. Thus A∪N(A) is closed under adjacency. Indeed, an edge from N(A) to a vertex outside A∪N(A) would put that vertex at distance two from a row of A. Connectivity gives V(G)=A∪N(A), and independence gives S=A. Now |V(G)|=|A|+|B|≤2k+2. We may therefore assume all supports have size at most k.

In each incidence component with s rows and b columns there are more than k(s−1) edges, since all but at most one row have degree at least k and the remaining row has positive degree. There are at most kb edges. Hence b≥s. The assumed global inequality forces equality in each component. Fix any component and denote its two part sizes by m. The component contains a row of degree at least k, since a component consisting of the sole exceptional row would have b≥2>s. Consequently m≥k.

Write d for the minimum of k and the smallest row degree in this component. Then d≥2. For a column C let its deficiency be k−|C|, with C also denoting its support. The sum D of all column deficiencies is at most k−d by edge counting. Equality forces every row besides a designated minimum-degree row to have degree exactly k if d<k. Call columns of size k full. At most k−2 columns are deficient, so each row of degree at least k belongs to some full support. At most one row z can therefore lie outside all full supports.

Two intersecting column supports have union of size at most k+1: a row in their intersection sees every other row of the union at distance two. Thus intersecting full supports overlap in at least k−1 rows. Intersecting is an equivalence relation on the full columns: transitivity follows since two subsets of a k-element middle support of size at least k−1 intersect for k≥3. Call the union of the supports of one equivalence class a cluster. The clusters are disjoint. Each cluster has size k or k+1. Indeed, fix a full support A in the class. A full support containing a row outside A contains at least k−1 rows of A. If there were two different outside rows, the corresponding sets of at least k−1 rows of A would intersect. Their common row would see both outsiders as well as the other k−1 rows of A at distance two.

We next show there is only one cluster. If a column C meets two clusters K and L, take a full support in each containing a row of C. The two full supports are disjoint. Intersecting each bounds the number of rows of C outside either support by one. Hence C has exactly two rows, one in each cluster. This single column uses k−2 units of deficiency. Thus d=2, all other columns are full, and every row other than the exceptional row has degree exactly k. Even the exceptional row belongs to a cluster because it is incident with at least two columns and only C is deficient. Choose one of K,L excluding that row. Its row degrees sum to a multiple of k. Counting the same incidences by columns gives a multiple of k from its full columns and precisely one extra incidence from C, a contradiction.

It remains to consider multiple clusters when every column meets at most one cluster. The component is connected, so different clusters must be joined through the unique row z outside all clusters. There are distinct columns P,Q containing z which meet different clusters. Both are deficient, and all their rows other than z belong to the corresponding clusters. The two sets of these rows are disjoint and are at distance two from z. Thus |P|+|Q|−2≤k. Their combined deficiency is at least k−2, forcing d=2, all other columns full, and all rows other than z of degree exactly k. For the cluster met by P, the sum of its row degrees is divisible by k. In the column count, full columns contribute a multiple of k and P contributes |P|−1, an integer between 1 and k−2. This contradiction proves the assertion.

The only cluster covers all rows except possibly z, so k≤m≤k+2.

* If m=k+2, the cluster has k+1 normal rows, with z the exceptional row outside it. Every two normal rows share a column, since each is incident with at least k among k+2 columns and 2k>k+2. Each normal row therefore sees its full quota of k distance-two vertices among normal rows. No column can contain both z and a normal row, contradicting connectedness of the incidence component.
* If m=k+1, any pair of rows shares a column: at least one is incident with k or more columns and the other with at least two. Every row has its k distance-two witnesses inside the row set R of this component. As before R∪N(R) is closed, contradicting connectivity and |V(G)|>2k+2.
* If m=k, every normal row is adjacent to every column. A normal row x therefore shares a neighbor with every other row, including a possible exceptional one. Any vertex outside R∪N(R) adjacent to a column is at distance two from x as well, so there is at most one such outside boundary vertex. All edges from R∪N(R) to its complement end at such a vertex. The complement has more than two vertices because |R∪N(R)|=2k. Connectivity forces the boundary to be nonempty, and deleting its sole vertex disconnects G. This contradicts 2-connectivity.

All cases are impossible. ∎

**Corollaries.** With open neighborhood defined as the union of the neighbors of the members, every nonempty proper vertex set U satisfies |N(U)|≥|U|+1. If A=U∖N(U) is nonempty, then A is independent and N(A)⊆N(U)∖U, giving the result by subtraction. If A is empty then U⊆N(U) and connectedness supplies an additional outside neighbor.

In the bipartite double cover, deleting any chosen left vertex and any chosen right vertex leaves a graph satisfying Hall's condition, hence possessing a perfect matching. Taking the two copies of the same original vertex v and projecting the resulting permutation to cycle components gives a perfect fractional matching of G−v. In this projection, two-cycles receive weight one on their edge and longer cycles weight one-half on each edge. Also, any chosen directed copy of an edge participates in a perfect matching of the full double cover, by deleting its endpoints and restoring the chosen edge. Averaging projections from one such perfect matching per edge gives a perfect fractional matching of G assigning strictly positive weight to every edge.

**Sharp order threshold.** For every k≥3, the crown graph obtained from the complete bipartite graph K_{k+1,k+1} by removing a perfect matching is k-regular and 2-connected. Two distinct vertices in one part have k−1 common neighbors and are at distance two. For vertices in opposite parts, all distances are odd. Thus every vertex has exactly k other vertices at positive even distance, while either bipartition class S satisfies |N(S)|=|S|. The graph has 2k+2 vertices.
