# Independent audit of the longest-path and cone argument

Let G be a finite simple 2-connected graph, let k≥3 be its second-smallest degree, and put δ=δ(G). Suppose every vertex has at most k other vertices at distance exactly two. A vertex of degree at least k is high. If n≤3k+2, there is a path containing every vertex other than a designated minimum-degree vertex z (possibly also containing z). In particular, G is traceable if 2δ>k or if n<(δ−1)(⌊k/2⌋+2)+4. The order-free longest-path omitted-component and attachment-gap conclusions in the rotation manuscript are valid as well.

## Longest paths

On any longest path, each omitted vertex's attachment indices are internal and nonconsecutive. For a nonempty set A of such indices with r runs of differences two, their predecessor/successor indices give |A|+r distinct distance-two witnesses. For adjacent omitted vertices x,y, a cross-difference one allows insertion of x,y. A cross-difference two replaces one path vertex with x,y. For paths required to pass through w only a replacement deleting w can fail. Overlapping indices in a nonsingleton run of A_x must therefore be absent in the unrooted case. In the rooted case only the one run adjacent on both sides of w may contribute, and at most the indices flanking w. The indices of A_y outside A_x give distinct further witnesses, since cross-difference one is forbidden. These observations verify the two inequalities |A_x|+|A_y|≤k−1 (unrooted) and ≤k+1 (rooted) when A_x contains a nonsingleton run.

A component of size m≤⌈k/2⌉ forces each high x in it to have at least k−m+1>k/2 attachments, hence a nonsingleton run. Two adjacent highs contradict the unrooted inequality. The remaining high-bearing component would be a star with low center, since an omitted high vertex needs an omitted neighbor. Any high leaf x has exactly k−1 attachments in a single run, using its entire distance-two budget. A second leaf is impossible. The center's attachments must belong to this run and any such one creates a forbidden cross-difference two. The resulting center has degree one, contradicting 2-connectivity. The rooted argument uses m≤⌊k/2⌋ and the sum of the two attachment counts ≥k+2, immediately contradicting the rooted inequality where all omitted vertices are high.

If a nontraceable G has a path on all vertices except z, take any two attachment indices of z differing by g. Replacing the intervening segment by z yields a path through z omitting g−1 high vertices. A longest such path omits at least ⌊k/2⌋+1, so g≥⌊k/2⌋+2. Thus δ internal attachment indices span at least (δ−1)(⌊k/2⌋+2) positions measured by differences, and at most n−4. All 2δ shifted indices are distinct, so 2δ≤k.

## Toughness for all k≥3

In a cone cut violating 1-toughness, let s be the number of additional G vertices removed and t the number of remaining components. Then s≥2 and t≥s+2. Each nontrivial component has at least two vertices touching S; every component has at least two neighbors in S. A high vertex in a component of order k−s+1 sees all of S. Every high vertex in a component of order ≤k sees at least one member of S. For X=V(G)∖{z}, α(G[X])≤k+2, since among k+3 independent vertices some two would have distance at least three and their disjoint neighborhoods would force n≥3k+3.

The cut bounds in longest-path.md are correct for k≥7. The following checks remove this restriction.

* If every component meets X then s≤k. In the range 2≤s≤k−2 the bound 4k−2 leaves only (k,s)=(4,2). Here four size-three components use all 14 vertices. A high vertex in one sees all of S and at least two vertices in each of the other three at distance two, exceeding k. For s=k−1 all t≥k+1 components have size at least two and at most one excess vertex is possible, so even for k=3 there is a minimum pair avoiding z. From a member, all other at least k nonsingletons each give at least two distance-two witnesses. The s=k singleton argument is unchanged.
* If z is a singleton then s≤k+1. For 3≤s≤k−2 the bound 4k−4 leaves (k,s)=(5,3),(6,3),(6,4). Each has s+1 high components of minimum size k−s+1 and respectively at most 1,0,0 excess vertices. Take a vertex in a minimum component, which sees all S. Each other high component has size ≤k and consists of vertices touching S. These components and z give at least s(k−s+1)+1>k witnesses. For s=k−1 at least k high components have order at least two, with at most two excess vertices relative to k pairs, so there is a minimum pair. From one of its members at least k−1 other nonsingletons each contribute two attachments and z contributes one. This works also for k=3. If s=2 and k≥4 there cannot be four high components because 3+4(k−1)>3k+2. The original proof then works without alteration. If s=2 and k=3 use s=k−1. The s=k,k+1 cases are unchanged.

## Li's theorem and cycle-to-path conversion

Jianping Li, “Cycles Containing Many Vertices of Subsets in 1-Tough Graphs with Large Degree Sums,” Ars Combinatoria 48 (1998), Theorem 8. Under 1-toughness and σ₃(X)≥|V(H)|, a cycle is simultaneously X-longest and X-dominating, meaning that every neighbor of each omitted vertex of X lies on that cycle. The publisher's original abstract gives the minimum-degree-sum definition and both properties. Its PDF also gives the quantitative bound with α(X); the quantitative bound is unnecessary here. https://combinatorialpress.com/article/ars/Volume%20048/volume-48-paper-18.pdf

In the cone H, σ₃(X)≥3(k+1)≥n+1=|V(H)| if an independent triple in X exists. If an X-longest, X-dominating cycle omits x∈X, it contains the universal vertex by domination. Delete that vertex. The G-path obtained then contains all the at least k original neighbors of x. The path can absorb x, since internal, nonconsecutive attachment indices would give at least k+1 distance-two witnesses. Reclose the enlarged path through the universal vertex. It has more X vertices, a contradiction. The cycle therefore covers X. Deleting the universal vertex, or just deleting a cycle edge if the universal vertex is absent, gives a path of G covering X. If X has no independent triple, α(H)≤3≤κ(H) so the Chvátal–Erdős theorem yields a Hamilton cycle of H directly.
