# Root degree and second-smallest degree both four

Follow-up manuscript. Independent review is pending.

Suppose \(G\) is a 2-connected qualifying graph with minimum and second-smallest degree four, with root \(z\) of degree four. The earlier [small-order theorem](degree-four.md) covers \(n\le14\). For \(n>14\), if \(G-N[z]\) is disconnected, the proof in [the three-port note](degree-four-three-ports.md) forces a component contained in \(T=N_2(z)\): the all-remote argument permits at most \(n=13\). The high-vertex distance bound applies to every vertex here. A remote-containing component has size at least five and has at least two ports by 2-connectivity. There is exactly one such component \(D\) because \(|T|\le4\), with at least one port in a pure component. If there were no remotes then \(n\le9\).

## Two ports in the remote component

If there are two pure singleton components, both singletons are adjacent to all anchors and there are exactly two ports \(u,v\) in \(D\). The root has no unused even slots, so every remote touches a port. There are at least six remotes. A shared anchor of the ports would see them all at distance two, impossible. The remaining anchor argument from [the two-port note](degree-four-two-ports.md) bounds each port's remote neighborhood by three, with equality and disjointness forced. Each port sees the root and both singleton vertices at distance two. It cannot be adjacent to the other port, since that would put the three other remotes at distance two. There is at most one cross edge between the two remote groups, since each uses an extra even slot at both ports. Every remote needs a cross edge to reach degree four, a contradiction.

If the single pure component \(K\) has size two, it is an edge and the root is again saturated. The same anchor bound forces two disjoint remote groups of size three, and \(|D|=8\). The marked-path closure proof from the two-port note works verbatim. For the shell routing, each of the two \(K\)-vertices misses at most one anchor. If all four anchors see \(K\), at most two anchors see only one \(K\)-vertex, and if two do so they see different vertices. Given endpoints \(a,b\) and interior anchors \(c,d\), order the interior anchors so that neither pair \((a,c),(d,b)\) consists of both singleton-support anchors, if they exist. Then each pair has a common \(K\)-neighbor, and distinct representatives exist because otherwise the other \(K\)-vertex would miss two anchors. Use these representatives as the two clique segments around \(c,z,d\). If an anchor sees no \(K\)-vertex, all other anchors are complete to \(K\) and the exceptional anchor has at least two anchor neighbors (its other possible neighbors are \(z\) and at most one port). If it is a prescribed endpoint, take its edge to a neighboring interior anchor and then use \(z\), the other interior anchor, \(K\), and the other endpoint. If it is interior, order the endpoints so the last is one of its anchor neighbors, and reach it via the other interior anchor and \(z\). Thus the marker expands as before.

If the sole pure component is a singleton and \(D\) has two ports, \(T\) has size three, so one far vertex is possible. There are at least seven remotes. The anchor bounds from the two-port note force the extreme configuration of two disjoint groups of size three and a far vertex. The same degree and even-slot argument gives a spanning path through \(D\) between its ports. In the shell every anchor has an anchor neighbor since each has at most one port contact and just two other potential neighbors, the root and the singleton. Given endpoints \(a,b\) and interior anchors \(c,d\), there is an anchor edge other than \(ab\). If it joins both interior anchors, use \(a,z,c,d,K,b\). If it joins an endpoint and an interior anchor, exchange labels and use \(a,c,z,d,K,b\). The two components can therefore be joined through the shell.

## Three ports in the remote component

The sole pure component is now a singleton complete to all anchors. Put \(P=\{p,q,r\}\) for the ports, \(C\) for the remotes, \(C_p\) for the remotes adjacent to \(p\), and \(B_p\) for the remotes at distance two from \(p\), with analogous definitions for the other ports. There are at least six remotes, all at distance three from the root. Exactly as in the three-port note, \(|C_p|\le3\), every private remote belongs to some \(B_p\), and \(|B_p|\le2\), reduced by one for each other port at distance two. For \(a\) private and \(t\) triple-visible remotes, \(a\ge2|C|-9+t\ge3+t\). Let \(e\) count distance-two port pairs, so \(a\le6-2e\).

If the ports form a triangle, \(a\le3\) by double-counting private remotes, forcing \(a=3,t=0,|C|=6\). The six incidences of private remotes exhaust the three \(B\) sets, but each double-visible remote is seen at distance two by the omitted port, a contradiction. If the ports form a path, the usual bounds give at most two private remotes of the ends together and at most one of the middle. Again equality \(a=3,t=0,|C|=6\) is forced, as well as \(|C_p|=3\) at every port. The middle's private remote uses both end ports' remaining slots. No double-visible remote can involve the middle, because the omitted end sees it at distance two through the middle. This contradicts the middle's required neighborhood size three.

Suppose the unique port edge is \(pq\). Every private remote belongs to \(B_p\cup B_q\), as in the three-port note. If one of \(p,q\) is at distance two from \(r\), this union has capacity three, so \(a=3,t=0,|C|=6\). It is filled by private remotes. A double-visible remote involving \(r\) and one of \(p,q\) is seen at distance two by the other, impossible. Then all three double-visible remotes share \(p,q\), and three private remotes of \(r\) must each see a double-visible remote, overfilling both \(B_p,B_q\). If neither \(p,q\) is distance two from \(r\), there are only \(pq\)-doubles, and \(a\le4\) forces \(|C|=6\). The same argument excludes \(a=3\). For \(a=4\), two double-visible remotes leave at most one private remote per port \(p,q\). All four private remotes fill \(B_p\cup B_q\) without duplication, so no private remote of \(r\) can touch a double-visible remote. If \(|C_r|=2\), each of its vertices needs two outside neighbors and must see the two singleton private remotes of \(p,q\), counting twice in \(B_p,B_q\). If \(|C_r|=3\), there is only one available private remote outside it, and all three vertices must see that remote, overfilling one of \(B_p,B_q\).

Finally suppose the ports are independent. If \(e\ge2\), \(a\le2\), impossible. If \(e=1\), all double-visible remotes share the unique distance-two pair \(p,q\), with no triples. Each private remote of \(r\) is in \(B_p\cup B_q\), which has capacity two. At least two of the six or more remotes are double-visible, since \(a\le4\). Three doubles would fill \(C_p,C_q\) and give at most five remotes in total. Thus there are two doubles, a private remote each at \(p,q\), and two private remotes at \(r\). Neither of the latter can see a double, since that would put it in both \(B_p,B_q\), whose two slots already cover the two private remotes at \(r\). Both need two outside neighbors, so must see both private remotes at \(p,q\), the same impossibility. If \(e=0\), all remotes are private and there are exactly six. Each can have outside neighbors in only one group, since each belongs to exactly one \(B\) set. All groups are nonempty by connectivity of \(D\), and each has size at most three. With sizes \((3,2,1)\), the singleton must see all of the largest group to reach degree four. None of these vertices can then have an outside neighbor in the middle group, which has no available outside neighbors. With sizes \((2,2,2)\), every vertex requires two neighbors in a single opposite group. Any crossing edge forces two whole groups to be paired and leaves the third group without outside neighbors.

This excludes the three-port case. Together with the two-port paths and the earlier small-order theorem, this would extend the degree-four neighborhood conclusion to \(k=4\).
