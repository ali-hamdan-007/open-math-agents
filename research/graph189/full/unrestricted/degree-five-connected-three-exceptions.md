# Three exceptional second-layer vertices when d=3 and the fourth layer is small

Use a unique degree-five root z with even cap and second-smallest degree k≥6 in the diameter-at-most-four regime at n>3k+2. Set A=N(z), T=L₂(z), R=L₃(z), F=L₄(z). Suppose |T|=k−3 and s=|F|≤2. Then A is a clique and each of its five vertices sees all of T except a pair drawn from three exceptional vertices. All three different pairs occur. Equivalently, T has k−6 vertices complete to A and three other vertices whose nonempty sets of anchor neighbors partition A.

Indeed h=|R|≥2k−s. No anchor covers T, and its degree bounds the number of its misses in T by two. An anchor missing two reaches its degree bound only by being adjacent to all other anchors; it then sees both missed T-vertices at distance two. An anchor missing one has an additional even witness outside R: a missed anchor, or its one missed T vertex through another anchor if complete to A. Thus an anchor missing m∈{1,2} vertices of T sees at most k−m members of R at distance two.

If two anchor miss-sets were disjoint, the pair would cover T and hence see all of R. Their miss sizes then sum to at most s≤2. This forces two singleton misses x,y, s=2, h=2k−2 and zero commonly visible members of R. Every member of T−{x,y} is adjacent to both anchors and therefore has no R-neighbor. Thus R splits into two disjoint supports S_x,S_y each of size k−1. Choose w∈T−{x,y}, possible because |T|−2=k−5≥1. Its possible neighbors are the five anchors and the other k−4 vertices of T, so it misses at most one of these. It cannot touch x and y together since it would see all of R at distance two. It therefore misses precisely one and sees all anchors. It sees z, the missed T vertex via an anchor, and all k−1 contacts of its adjacent special port at distance two, an impossibility.

The five nonempty miss-sets are thus pairwise intersecting but have empty total intersection. A singleton would belong to all sets; hence they all have size two. Choose two distinct sets {a,b} and {a,c}. Some set misses a, and must equal {b,c} to meet both. Any two-set intersecting those three is one of those same three. This proves the template.

For k=6 this template is impossible. Here T is exactly the exceptional triple, with support sizes |S_i|≤4, and anchor multiplicities a_i≥1 summing to five. Port i sees z and all 5−a_i opposite anchors at distance two, leaving at most a_i even slots. Incidence counting forces at least 2h−12≥12−2s private core vertices having a unique port. For s≤1, every private vertex needs a core neighbor outside its own support, since it has at most four neighbors within its support plus the ports and at most one additional neighbor in F. An outside core neighbor puts the private vertex into an opposite port's even slots. The total number of these available slots is at most five, a contradiction.

For s=2, each f∈F has at least five core contacts, since only the other f can be a neighbor outside R. Those contacts meet at least two port supports, so the two fourth-layer vertices spend at least four of the five slots in total. Among the at least eight private vertices, at most one has an outside-support core neighbor. Each of the at least seven remaining private vertices therefore needs both members of F as neighbors to reach degree six. Its own port must have multiplicity at least two. These private contacts cannot fit into a single support of size at most four. The multiplicities must be 2,2,1, with each of the two larger groups spending both its port slots on F. Since h≥10, the two large ports' supports cannot cover R, so there is a vertex private to the third port. Such a vertex cannot have a core neighbor outside its own support, since that would spend a slot at a large port. Nor can it meet both fourth-layer vertices, as its own port has just one slot. Its degree is at most five, a contradiction. Hence at k=6, |T|=3 in a high-order residual forces |F|=3.
