# Circular-arc separators and the scope of the path certificate

## Check of the circular separator argument

For an inclusion-minimal violating separator S, the touched components per anchor form a consecutive circular range. The unions of the arcs in the components are connected proper circular intervals, mutually disjoint. One can use a representation with all endpoints distinct. If an anchor touches fewer than all component unions, lift its arc to a real interval. Traversing it from end to end encounters the touched components consecutively, and all except the first and last are completely covered. If it touches every component and its two endpoints are in different component unions, those two are consecutive around the complementary gap, since no complete component can be missed. If its two endpoints lie in the same component union, all others are completely covered. If an endpoint lies in a gap, the first or last component can be chosen simply to overestimate the set of possible partial contacts. Thus a cyclic listing with at most two endpoint exceptions always exists, including for a whole-circle anchor.

Each anchor touches at least three components. An anchor complete to two high-containing components would give mutually inconsistent strict component-size inequalities by choosing a high vertex of each of those components. Choose a special index zero for the possible exceptional component. Thus every range has length three or four, with a length-four range required to have index zero as one of its adjacent two internal positions. In fact such a zero component contains no high vertex.

For c≥5, consider a length-three range centered at a far i, of cyclic distance ≥2 from zero. Any other anchor touching i must either have the same length-three range, or have i at an endpoint. Indeed a length-four range has no far internal position. If i is at an endpoint, its immediately adjacent internal position j is different from zero and is complete to this new anchor. The anchor centered at i touches j, the new anchor touches i, and both components i and j consist entirely of high vertices. The two strict size inequalities thus contradict each other. Consequently all anchors touching i must have the same triple. There are at least two such anchors by 2-connectivity. At least four components must touch their union, by applying the minimal-subset inequality to the complement of their two-element anchor set. This excludes the far center. All ranges are therefore within cyclic distance two of zero, forcing c≤5. At c=5 the two extreme distance-two indices can never lie in the same allowed range, and require at least four anchors between them, whereas c≥s+2 forces s≤3. At c=4 both anchors meet all components. Each completes the low singleton and some high-containing interior component H. The singleton reaches all vertices of H and a witness in each other component at distance two, whereas a high H vertex has degree at most |H|+1. This verifies the claimed separator inequality.

## The separator bound alone does not imply traceability, even in this class

Let A₁,A₂,A₃ be three disjoint pairs, whose six vertices induce a clique. Adjoin two nonadjacent vertices for each i, both with neighborhood precisely Aᵢ. This graph is 2-connected. It is circular-arc: on a circle of circumference 12, put two equal length-five arcs centered at each of 0, 4, and 8. Inside each pair's private central region put two disjoint short arcs for its two additional vertices. Small perturbations can make all endpoints distinct. In a spanning path both vertices private to Aᵢ cannot be internal: they would have to use every edge of the cycle K₂,₂ on their two neighbors, which a path cannot do. Each of the three pairs therefore requires a different path endpoint. There is no spanning path.

If a deletion set S leaves one of the six core vertices, exactly one surviving component contains the remaining core vertices. Every other component is an isolated private vertex, possible only if its two core neighbors were deleted. Their disjoint grouping gives at most |S| such isolated vertices. If all six core vertices are deleted there are at most six components. Thus c(G−S)≤|S|+1 for every S. The second-smallest degree is 2, and a private vertex sees its sibling and four outside core vertices at distance two, so this example fails the Graph 189 hypothesis.

In the 2011 Hung–Chang circular-arc certifying paper, Section 5 verifies a negative Hamiltonicity certificate by removing its cut from a modified arc family in which selected arcs can be shortened. Only one branch leaves the entire family unchanged. Section 6 gives the universal-vertex path-cover reduction and an algorithm for determining a path, but does not replace the modified-family certificate by an original-graph scattering obstruction. The example above supplies a direct reason the latter strengthening is unavailable. See R.-W. Hung and M.-S. Chang, “An efficient certifying algorithm for the Hamiltonian cycle problem on circular-arc graphs”, Theoretical Computer Science 412 (2011), 5351–5373, §§5–6.
