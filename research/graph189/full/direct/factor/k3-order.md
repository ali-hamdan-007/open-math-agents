# The tied degree-three case has order at most eleven

Suppose the minimum and second-smallest degrees of a connected qualifying graph are both three. The established high-pair distance lemma gives diameter at most four. Fix a degree-three vertex z, with its three anchors A=N(z), and successive layers B,C,D. Then |B|+|D|≤3. We show |C|≤4, proving n≤11=3k+2 and hence traceability by the existing small-order theorem. With the separate result for minimum degree at most two, this covers second-smallest degree three.

A vertex c∈C may be allocated to any one B-neighbor. Let each B-column carry the count of vertices allocated to it, and say a positive count is active. The columns seen by an anchor a contribute all their allocated vertices at distance two from a. Every other anchor not adjacent to a also costs a distance-two witness via z. Finally any missed B-column meeting an anchor adjacent to a costs another distance-two witness. No column is empty of anchors.

If A is independent, each of its three anchors has room for only one C vertex, giving |C|≤3. If A has exactly one edge, the two ends have room for at most two apiece and the isolated anchor for one. Equality |C|=5 would require these three nonempty C sets to be disjoint. Each anchor would then need a private B column to reach any such C vertex. With |B|≤3 all columns would be private. Either end of the anchor edge then misses the other end's private column but sees it at distance two, reducing its C allowance below two, a contradiction.

Suppose A is a three-vertex path with middle anchor m. Each end anchor has room for at most two C vertices. Let t count the columns missed by m. All are at distance two from m via an end anchor, so the columns meeting m together have at most 3−t allocations. If t≤1, the remaining t columns each have count at most two through an end anchor, giving total at most four. If all columns miss m, every C vertex is counted by at least one of the two ends, again giving at most four. The sole remaining possibility is t=2 with exactly one column meeting m. To reach a total of five, that column must have one allocation and the other two must together have four allocations. These four already use all two-plus-two places at the end anchors. Hence neither end can touch the column meeting m, since each would then see the fifth C vertex as well. But both ends then see that missed column itself at distance two through m, reducing both allowances. This is impossible.

Finally let A be a triangle and let s≤3 be the number of active columns. Fix an active column j and one of its incident anchors a. Among the other s−1 active columns, each either touches a and contributes at least one allocated C vertex, or misses a and is itself at distance two via one of the other anchors. These are s−1 distinct witnesses in addition to the allocations of j. Thus j has at most 4−s allocations and |C|≤s(4−s)≤4. This completes the proof. No enumeration is needed.
