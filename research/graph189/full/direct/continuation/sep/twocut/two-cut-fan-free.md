# Graph 189 at connectivity two and degree at least eleven

Let G satisfy the original Graph 189 hypotheses. Suppose the second-smallest degree is k≥11 and the vertex connectivity is two. The proof below gives a Hamiltonian path using longest-path rotations, Dirac and Ore on dense sides, and the original Graph 189 path gap. It does not require a circumference lower bound. This is a paper assembly of the separating-pair case. Its status by dependency is at the end.

## Cut and counting facts

Write h=ceil(k/2) and call a vertex high if its degree is at least k. At most one vertex is low. A counterexample has minimum degree at least three by the degree-two theorem. The checked index-three estimate gives n≥4k−5, so the checked edge-order estimate forces every edge into a triangle. The original path gap makes a path missing at most h+1 vertices sufficient.

Choose a minimum-population side A among all genuine size-two cuts, and let B be its opposite side. Write p,q for the two anchors and a,b for the side orders, so a≤b. Both sides are internally connected and have at least two vertices by the checked least-cut selector. Every anchor meets both sides. Each side has at least one high vertex and hence size at least k−1. A port meets an anchor, a common port meets both and a private port meets only one. Let l_A,l_B count low vertices of the respective sides. Then l_A+l_B≤1. Distances below are in the original graph.

1. Every opposite contact of p counts all p-contacts of the other side at distance two, so each such contact count is at most k. If the witness is private and the count is k, saturation forces it adjacent to all other vertices of its own connected side: an internal nonneighbor gives an additional internal distance-two vertex. It then sees q through an internal q-contact, a contradiction. Thus a private witness bounds the opposite contact count by k−1. A common port bounds the entire opposite port count by k.
2. Two high vertices are at distance at most four. If one side contains a high nonport, every high nonport in the opposite side is at distance four from it. The fourth layer reserves at least two second-layer vertices by 2-connectivity, so that opposite side has at most k−2 high nonports. More generally a high vertex opposite at least k−1 high vertices lies within distance two of an anchor. Otherwise all those high vertices are at distance four and its second layer adds at least two.
3. If a vertex of one side touches every anchor two steps from a high nonport of its own side, the opposite side has size at most 2k−2 plus its low count. One row counts the contacts sharing its labels, and the nonport's row counts all remaining high opposite vertices at distance four with the two-place second-layer debit. Thus opposite size at least 2k forces every high nonport to be at distance two from both anchors, with no common port on its side. The same holds opposite a wholly high side of size 2k−1.
4. A high p-private port at distance at least three from q also bounds the opposite side by 2k−2 plus its low count, provided its own side has a noncontact of p. The p-row counts opposite vertices at distance two, reserving a local witness from a path to the noncontact. The private port has a local second-layer witness because it misses every local q-contact. It sees every high opposite vertex outside p's second layer at distance two or four. These two rows give the claimed bound. In particular, any high private representative in a side having both types has distance two to its missing anchor when the opposite side is large or wholly high of size 2k−1.

## An ordinary longest-path rule for side contacts

Let D be either cut component. Within D a high nonport has degree at least k. A high port has degree at least k−1 on a common-free side and at least k−2 in general. If the number of high nonports plus the possible low vertex is at most k, the following gives a longest side path starting at a high port.

Take a longest path with both endpoints high. If the only low vertex is an endpoint of internal degree at least two, rotate at a neighbor other than its successor to change that endpoint. If both high endpoints are nonports, pick the initial one and keep the far endpoint. Every internal neighbor of the initial vertex lies on the path, and rotating at it gives its predecessor as the new initial endpoint. The k or more predecessors are distinct and omit the fixed far endpoint, which is itself forbidden. Thus some predecessor is a high port.

A low vertex of internal degree one must be a common port of original degree three. Delete the low leaf. Its neighbor r is the only remaining vertex whose degree dropped, and still has degree at least k−3. Rotate away r if it is an endpoint. If both resulting endpoints are nonports and distinct from r, each still has internal degree at least k. Take the forbidden set to be the nonports plus r if r is a port. It has at most k vertices by the assumed count. Again a rotation chooses a high port distinct from r.

In a connected graph of order m, a longest path with endpoint degrees d₁,d₂ has at least min(m,d₁+d₂+1) vertices. If a shifted left-end neighbor meets the right-end neighbor set, the two edges close a cycle on the path and connectedness would extend it unless it spans. Otherwise the two sets are disjoint in the path with its last vertex removed. Thus the contact path has at least min(|D|,2k−1) vertices when D is common-free. For a side with common ports the bound is min(|D|,2k−3), or min(|D|−1,2k−3) if the low leaf is removed. On a balanced side of order at most 2k−1, this leaves at most two omissions. On a large side of order at least 2k, deleting the leaf leaves the required length 2k−3.

## Large opposite side, b≥2k

Suppose first A is common-free. Both private types have high representatives, by taking a triangle at an anchor-contact edge, since its third vertex must be in A. By the metric bounds, all high A ports are at distance two from their missing anchors, and every high A nonport is at distance two from both. A high vertex loses at most one anchor neighbor, so a≥k. Every high B nonport has a nearest anchor at distance two.

Write w for the number of high B nonports at distance two from both anchors, v for the number singly near, t for the common B port count and l for the low B nonport count. The private-representative rows give b≤2k−2+w+l−t: each row counts its missing anchor, the opposite ports of its label, and the high nonports singly near its missing label, the last at distance four. Both anchor rows give a−l_A+v+2w+2j≤2k, with j=1 if B has a common port and the anchors are nonadjacent, and j=0 if B is common-free. If anchors are adjacent and B has a common port, all high vertices lie within distance two of an anchor. The inclusive even rows miss only the common ports and possibly one low vertex. An A private port counts q and all common B ports at distance two, so t≤k−1 and n≤3k+2, impossible.

The two displayed inequalities imply a≤2k−2 if A is wholly high and a≤2k−3 otherwise, using w≥2−l+t. Hence from either anchor A has a path losing at most l_A by Dirac in A or A minus its low vertex. If only the low vertex meets the anchor, start there and enter the high cycle at an internal neighbor. They also imply v+w+l_B≤k when j=0 and v+w+l_B≤k−3 when j=1, using a≥k, w≥2−l+t and l_A+l_B≤1. The ordinary longest-path rule therefore gives B path length at least 2k−1−2j starting at a port. The arithmetic bound 2b≤5k−4+l_A+2l−4j leaves at most h+1 total omissions including the unused anchor.

If A has a common port, every high A vertex is a port, by the metric bounds and b≥2k. Suppose both private types occur in A. A high representative is at distance two from its missing anchor by the far-contact bound. A low-only representative likewise touches its missing anchor in two steps through a high internal neighbor. At least one type is high, so a≥k and high B nonports are near an anchor. Let c count common A ports and let L=l_A+l_B. The common A contact gives at most k B ports. The private rows again give b≤2k−2+w+l−t. If pq is an edge, the inclusive even rows give n+w≤2k+3+c+t. With w≥2−l+t and c≤k−1, this gives n≤3k+1. If pq is absent, its distance is two via an A common port. The anchor rows and B port bound give n+w≤3k+c+L, while a≤2k−c+l_A. Combining gives 2n≤7k+2−t. When B is common-free its private contacts strengthen the A port counts to a≤2k−2−c+l_A, giving 2n≤7k. If t≥1, the only possible case at k≥11 is equality at k=11,t=1. Equality forces both A contact counts to be k and the B port union to have size k. The saturation argument forces all B contacts to be common, contrary to t=1.

The remaining large-side shape lacks one private type in A. Rename anchors so every high vertex of A touches p and there is no q-private port. The A p-contact count is at most k. If it equals k, any B p-contact is internally universal, giving b≤2k from the p-row plus at most k B p-contacts. Since a≤k+1, this contradicts n≥4k−5. If the count is at most k−2, then A has exactly k−1 vertices, including a low nonport. All its high vertices are common to attain degree k. A high common vertex and the low nonport have opposite distance parities to every B vertex, and the low nonport also sees both anchors at distance two. Thus b≤2k−2, impossible. Exactly k−1 p-contacts remain, so a≤k and b≥3k−7>2k+1. The checked original-graph shallow recut theorem applies with the minimum choice of A and the triangle hypothesis, giving n≤3k+2.

## Balanced sides, a,b≤2k−1

In this range both sides have order at least 2k−6, since n≥4k−5. Every high vertex in either side has a nearest anchor within distance two. At least one side is wholly high. Side labels may be exchanged in the following arguments because the minimum side is only needed by the large shallow recut.

### Both sides are common-free

Each private class has size at most k−1 by the opposite private-contact bound. Suppose a wholly high side D has order at most 2k−2. The internal minimum degree of D is at least k−1, so Dirac yields a spanning path from a contact of either selected anchor. The opposite side E satisfies the longest-path rule. If D has a high nonport, E has at most k−2 high nonports. If D is shallow, every D private is within distance two of its missing anchor since its k−1 internal neighbors cannot all lie in its private class of size at most k−1. Thus the anchor rows count every member of D and leave at most six high nonports of E. With the possible low vertex, at most seven vertices of E are forbidden endpoint choices. The resulting path in E spans, since it is common-free of order at most 2k−1. Join the paths through its selected anchor.

If both sides are wholly high and both have order 2k−1, the strict far-private and nearest-label bounds force every vertex of both sides to count at an anchor's second layer. Their combined orders exceed 2k, impossible. The remaining case has a wholly high side D of order 2k−1 and a low vertex in E. The high side D contains a high nonport, since its two private classes total at most 2k−2.

If |E|≤2k−3, deleting its low vertex leaves order at most 2k−4 and minimum internal degree at least k−2. Dirac gives a path from either anchor losing at most one, by the same low-contact argument as on the small side above. The path in D from some port follows from the longest-path rule. If E has a high nonport, D has at most k−2 high nonports. Otherwise every high E vertex is private and is two steps from its missing anchor, by the strict far-port bound against wholly high D at 2k−1. These |E|−1≥2k−7 incidences leave at most seven high D nonports.

If |E|≥2k−2, the strict metric bounds make every high E vertex count in an anchor's second layer, because D is wholly high of order 2k−1. The two rows leave at most three places for high D nonports and private D vertices near their missing anchor. Every high D nonport uses at least one. Every end of an edge between D's two private classes is near its missing anchor and also uses a place. Thus both private classes have internal minimum degree at least k−4: a vertex loses at most one neighbor to the anchors and at most three to nonports or the other class. Each class has at most k−1 vertices and at least two vertices, so each is Hamilton-connected by Ore. A shortest link between the classes in connected D has only nonports internally. Open a spanning path of the chosen initial class at any vertex different from its link end, traverse its link and then span the other class. This gives a D path from a contact of either prescribed anchor missing at most three. As D has a high nonport, the ordinary longest-path rule gives a spanning E path from some port. Matching D to it through that anchor misses at most four vertices.

### Exactly one side has common ports

Let C have common ports and D be common-free. D has a high nonport, since otherwise the opposite common port would give |D|≤k+1, contrary to |D|≥2k−6. C has a high nonport as well. If C were shallow and lacked one private type, all its high vertices would meet one anchor, giving |C|≤k+1. If it were shallow with both types, opposite D private contacts would bound each C label count by k−1. Each high C private is then two steps from its missing anchor: otherwise its internal neighbors would all be in its private class of size at most k−2, or at the one low nonport. This cannot supply k−1 internal neighbors. Any low-only representative is also two steps from the missing anchor by an internal high neighbor. The census for shallow C with two private types now applies without the assumption b≥2k. For adjacent anchors, n≤2k+3+c≤3k+2 since C's common count c is at most k−1. For nonadjacent anchors, the bound 2n≤7k applies because D is common-free. Both contradict n≥4k−5.

If D is wholly high, an opposite common port and an opposite high nonport bound |D|≤k+(k−2)=2k−2. Dirac spans D from a contact of either anchor. From C, use the ordinary longest-path rule, since D's high nonport limits the high nonports in C. It loses at most two vertices. If D contains the low vertex and |D|≤2k−3, Dirac after deleting that vertex loses at most one from D, from whichever anchor the C path selects. In this case C is wholly high and its contact route likewise loses at most two.

It remains that D contains the low vertex and |D|≥2k−2. Then C is wholly high and |C|≤2k−2. Indeed, if |C|=2k−1, every high D vertex is charged to an anchor and every high D nonport to both by the strict metric bounds. A common C port bounds the D ports by k, so there are at least k−3 high D nonports. The anchor rows would count at least (2k−3)+(k−3)>2k. This eliminates |C|=2k−1.

If |C|≤2k−4, Dirac spans C from either anchor. Otherwise |C|≥2k−3. The high nonport in C bounds the high D nonports by k−2, so the D ports number at least k−1, allowing the low vertex. Hence each common vertex of C can have at most one internal second-layer vertex. In fact it misses at most one other vertex of C. If the internal second layer consists of a single gate and a vertex lies beyond it, every vertex beyond the gate misses both anchors, since a contact would have distance two from the common vertex and exceed its cap. The gate alone then separates those further vertices from the other side in G, impossible.

Delete any common C vertex c₀. The remaining common vertices have degree at least |C|−3≥k−2, and the remaining noncommon vertices also have internal degree at least k−2. Since the remaining order is at most 2k−3, Ore traceability supplies a spanning path. The deleted c₀ misses at most one vertex and can attach to one end of this path. This yields a spanning C path starting at a common contact, available for either anchor. The ordinary longest-path rule on D supplies a path from some port, since C has a high nonport. The two sides match.

### Both sides have common ports

Each side has a high nonport because an opposite common port bounds its ports by k and its size is at least 2k−6>k+1. Choose a wholly high side D. Its high vertices consist of at most k ports and k−2 nonports by the two opposite witnesses, so |D|≤2k−2. The ordinary longest-path rule gives a path from some contact in the other side, losing at most two vertices. To match it, fix the anchor q it uses.

If |D|≤2k−3, the graph on D plus q has every D vertex of degree at least k−1. Hence it satisfies rooted local Ore with root q and has a spanning path starting at q. If |D|=2k−2, adjoin q and a leaf at q to D, forming a graph of order 2k. Ordinary Hamiltonian-path closure permits all missing edges from every D vertex missing p to every other D vertex: one of the two degrees is at least k, the other at least k−1. After these closures, the vertices missing p are universal within D. If there are at least k−1 of them, alternate k−1 chosen universal vertices with the other k−1 vertices of D. Start at any q-contact, choosing its part as needed.

Otherwise at least k vertices of D touch p. Since D has at most k ports, all its k ports touch p and the k−2 nonports are universal in D after closure. There is an original edge between two D ports. If not, a common port sees the other k−1 D ports at distance two via p and also at least two opposite ports at distance two, exceeding its quota. The opposite side must have at least two ports, because a sole port would separate the rest of that side. Order the ports starting with a chosen q-contact and place the two ends of the original edge consecutively, then insert the k−2 universal nonports in the other gaps. This spans D in the closure. Prepend q and its leaf. Reversing the closure retains a Hamiltonian path in the original augmented graph, and the added leaf forces that path to start through q and enter at an original q-contact. This proves the matching route in D. Joining through q misses at most three vertices overall.

This exhausts the separating-pair cases at k≥11.
