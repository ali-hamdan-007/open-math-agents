# A separating pair of degree k forces traceability

Let k be the second-smallest degree of a finite connected graph in which every vertex has at most k other vertices at positive even distance. Suppose the graph has a separating pair p,q with d(p)=d(q)=k. Then the graph has a Hamiltonian path, with no additional condition on the minimum or maximum degree. In particular a counterexample with exactly one degree k−1 and every other degree k cannot have connectivity two.

We use the earlier cut-vertex, adjacent-separating-pair, three-or-more-component, minimum-degree-two, and bounded-order (n≤3k+2) theorems. We can assume a 2-connected counterexample where p,q are nonadjacent and their deletion leaves exactly two components. Write a port for a vertex adjacent to an anchor p or q, call it common when adjacent to both and private otherwise, and call nonports in a side deep vertices. The companion shallow-high-two-cut.md handles instances with high vertices only in the components, and shallow-low-two-cut.md handles instances where the low vertex lies on a side consisting entirely of ports. Every edge in a counterexample belongs to a triangle, by the previously checked edge order theorem. Hence each anchor has at least two contacts in each component: the triangle on any anchor–contact edge supplies another same-component contact since pq is absent. At least one such contact on each side is high.

## A side contains only ports

It suffices to treat a high shallow D opposite a deep side C containing the low vertex. If C contains a common port, the first lemma of shallow-high-two-cut.md gives small order. Otherwise C has private ports only and each anchor has at most k−1 contacts in D (a private opposite port seeing k D contacts would be internally universal and would see its missing anchor at distance two). If D lacks one of its private types it is K_{k−1}, all common, and the last argument of the same companion gives small order even with a low vertex in C. Thus D has both private types. Write d=|D|, t for its number of common ports, r for the number of C nonports, and w for the number of high C nonports at distance two from both anchors.

A high private D vertex has at least k−1 internal neighbors, so d≥k. Every such private sees the opposite anchor at distance two, since its own private class has at most k−1 members. Each high C nonport has depth two from at least one anchor: otherwise it sees all d high D vertices at distance four (the high-pair distance bound is four) as well as at least two second-layer vertices. The anchor degree sum gives n=2+2k−t+r. Assume n≥3k+3, hence r≥k+t+1. Together the even quotas at p and q count at least r−1+w, the d−t private D ports, and two further places via their mutual distance two if t>0. If t>0 this implies r≤k+t−1, impossible. At t=0 equality is forced, with d=k,r=k+1,w=0. A p-private D port sees all C p-contacts, q itself, and every high C nonport with unique nearest anchor q: the latter has exact distance four since entry via p costs at least 1+3 and entry via q costs 2+2. The symmetric counts give |C|≤2k−2+w+1=2k−1, allowing for the one exceptional nonport. Yet there are 2k−d=k C ports and k+1 nonports. This contradiction covers shallow sides.

## Both sides are deep and there are no common ports

For an anchor u, choose any high u-contact on each side. Its closed neighborhood inside its component has at least k members, since u is its only possible outside neighbor. The two disjoint closed neighborhoods consist only of vertices at distance one or two from u. Since d(u)=k and its even cap is k, they exhaust both layers, with each set of size exactly k. Write U_u(S) for the corresponding set on side S. Every high u-contact in S has precisely U_u(S) as its internal closed neighborhood. The internal closed neighborhood of every other u-contact is contained in U_u(S), by the same layer equality. The anchor has no fourth layer, since all even places are used at distance two. As p,q have no common neighbor, they are at distance three. A side X therefore contains an edge between a p-contact and a q-contact.

On this side every high p-contact is adjacent to every high q-contact. Indeed the q-end of the crossing edge lies in U_p(X), so it neighbors all high p-contacts. Each high p-contact consequently belongs to U_q(X), and hence neighbors all high q-contacts. This uses the closed-neighborhood containment even when an endpoint of the initial crossing edge is low. Every high port of X is at distance two from its opposite anchor. Fix a high p-contact e and high q-contact f in X.

We may choose X so that no high nonport in X is at distance ≥3 from both anchors. This holds whenever the opposite side Y has a high nonport, by the high-pair distance bound. If one side's only nonport is the sole low vertex, choose this side as X. It contains a crossing edge: otherwise the internal closed neighborhood of one of its high p-contacts has at most the size of its private p-class plus one lone nonport. Its size k would require at least k−1 p-contacts there, conflicting with at least two on the opposite side. On the selected side X its only nonport in this case is low.

Count positive-even places at p,q,e,f, with total budget 4k. Among the 2k private ports, those on Y are seen at distance two by e or f according to their type. Each high port on X is seen at distance two from its opposite anchor. The two opposite-anchor distances from e,f supply two additional places. Every high nonport contributes at least twice, as follows.

- Distance two from both anchors contributes twice directly.
- On X, a nonport whose sole nearest anchor is q is seen once from q and also at distance two from e. Indeed its q-contact neighbor has closed neighborhood contained in U_q(X); thus it is adjacent to every high q-contact, each adjacent to e. The nonport misses e because it misses the p side at distance two. Exchange p,q for the other orientation.
- On Y, a nonport at distance two only from q is at distance four from e: the route through p costs at least 1+3, and through q at least 2+2. The upper bound is realized through q, and q itself sees it at distance two. The reverse case is symmetric.
- A high nonport in Y of depth at least three is at distance at least four from e,f and exactly four by the high-pair bound. No high nonport in X has this depth by the choice of X.

If the unique low vertex is a port, at most one port charge is lost; if it is a nonport, at most two nonport charges are lost and there is no lost port charge. Thus, writing r_total for all nonports, we have 4k≥2k+2+2r_total−2. It follows that r_total≤k and n=2+2k+r_total≤3k+2. This proves the case without common ports.

## Both sides are deep and a common port exists

If both sides have a common port and both have a high nonport, apply the prior common-port theorem in adjacent-separator.md, section “Removing the equal-minimum-degree restriction.” Its nonadjacent alternative is included in its statement. If a side C's only nonport is the unique low vertex z and the opposite D has a common port h, then |P_C|≥k−2 because a high C vertex has at most two neighbors outside C, and |P_C|≤k since h sees every C port at distance two. At |P_C|=k−2 the side has exactly k−1 vertices, so each high vertex is common and universal in C to attain degree k. Every neighbor of z is one of those common vertices, and every one of them is adjacent to z. Choose any such vertex u. For every vertex v on the opposite side, d(u,v)=1+d(v,{p,q}) and d(z,v)=2+d(v,{p,q}). Exactly one of their distances to v is even. Both anchors are at distance two from z, so the opposite side has at most 2k−2 vertices. This gives |G|≤3k−1. At |P_C|≥k−1, as in the shallow-common lemma, h's 2-connected torso on D,p,q has at most k−|P_C|≤1 vertices missing from its closed neighborhood. At p, all D vertices missing p except these exceptions are seen at distance two, as well as q via h. Also some original p-contact in C witnesses |N_D(p)|≤k. Hence |D|≤3k−|P_C|−1 and the total order is 2+(|P_C|+1)+|D|≤3k+2. This covers all cases where both sides have common ports.

Finally suppose C has no common port and only D has one. A high p-contact in C is private. No high p-contact of D can be private: its internal closed neighborhood and that of the high private C contact would already consume all 2k first-/second-layer positions at p, with q at distance two as an additional vertex. Similarly no high q-contact of D is private. Because each anchor has at least two D contacts, some high common D contact h exists. For either anchor p or q, the high private opposite contact and h have internal closed neighborhoods of at least k and k−1 vertices, respectively. The other anchor is a further vertex in the second layer. Saturation follows: the D vertices at distance one or two from either anchor constitute the very same set N_D[h] of size k−1.

If C has no high nonport, its only nonport is the low vertex, and the preceding opposite-common order argument applies. Otherwise every high D nonport has depth two from some anchor by the opposite high nonport, so every high D vertex belongs to N_D[h]. Thus |D|≤k. There cannot be a high D nonport since such a vertex needs k neighbors inside D. Since D is deep, its only nonport is the low z. All its neighbors are ports, so z also belongs to N_D[h], since it is at distance two from some anchor. This forces |D|=k−1, so all its high vertices are common and universal in D to attain degree k. The immediately preceding parity argument (with the sides exchanged) bounds the opposite component by 2k−2 vertices. The order theorem finishes the proof.
