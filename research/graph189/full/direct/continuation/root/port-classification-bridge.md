# How the even-distance condition restricts the smaller side's contacts

Let S split G into nonempty anticomplete sides A and B covering G−S. Put k equal to the second-smallest degree; a vertex of degree at least k is high. Let Y be the high vertices of B. Assume |Y|>|S|k and the Graph 189 even-distance hypothesis. At a minimum cut, the high vertices in A have one of only two contact patterns: after removing the common labels and restricting to labels that occur, every support has at most one label, or every support misses at most one label. This is the structural dichotomy needed in the 102-interval proof.

Fix a high x in A, any y in A, and let T,U be their respective sets of neighbors in S. Suppose y adds two or more labels to T. Use x, y, the anchors in T, and one representative on the A side for each label in S outside T∪U. There are at most |S| such roots. A minimum separator supplies all these representatives. They can be low.

To see that the roots cover Y at positive even distance, consider v in Y. If x is at even distance from v, use x. Otherwise the previously proved diameter bound for high pairs gives distance three: the sides are anticomplete. A geodesic crosses a label j. If that label is its first step, j belongs to T and lies at distance two from v. If the label is its second step it touches v. Its representative in A is x, y, or one of the selected representatives, according as j lies in T, in U, or in neither. That representative has distance two from v, since crossing the sides takes at least two edges. All roots are outside B. Summing their positive-even budgets gives |Y|≤|S|k, a contradiction. This proves |U−T|≤1 for each ordered pair of high A vertices. The estimate works even when y is low; the distance-four input only involves x and v.

The last step is purely set theoretic. If each set has at most one element outside the common core the first alternative holds. Otherwise choose P with two elements x,y missing somewhere, and choose Q missing x and R missing y. Then Q contains every element of P except x, and R every element of P except y. Since y lies in Q−R and x in R−Q, Q and R agree outside P. If z outside P occurs anywhere, some T contains it. If Q and R omit z, the one-difference property forces T to omit both x and y, which is impossible. Thus z belongs to both Q and R, and there is at most one such z since |Q−P|≤1. A member omitting z must then contain every v in P: at least one of Q,R contains v and z. Every member therefore omits at most one element of the union. This also handles an empty family or repeated supports.

A separate local consequence of the same even budget supplies the high representatives used later for private-support counts. If a port x has an edge whose first step shortens distances to two anchors, choose that neighbor as the first step for both and any geodesic first step for every other anchor. With x itself there are at most |S| roots whose even-distance sets cover B. Thus such a step is impossible when |B|>|S|k. When the order exceeds (|S|+1)k+|S|+1, the edge-order theorem forces any label touching just the unique low vertex on A to touch every other label. A different high port sharing another label would then have a step shortening both anchor distances. A two-connected graph supplies such a high port on any side of size at least two by deleting the possible low port and following a walk to B. A singleton side is excluded by its own root and all the anchors, which give the order bound (|S|+1)k+|S|+1. The resulting high representative exists separately for each label. It need not be the same high vertex for all labels.
