# The remaining exterior cycles at degree six

This is a paper argument for the original conjecture at second-smallest degree six and every order. It specializes the longest-path rotation, terminal closure and two-mark exchange in the full proof. The pinned kernel has verified the six-cycle exclusion, the five-cycle with five or six omissions, and the component and tail lemmas for the remaining seven to nine omissions. The final integration is being replayed. The moderate theorem handles $n\le20$, the cut-vertex theorem handles connectivity one, and the degree-two theorem permits assuming $\delta\ge3$. The rooted layer bound gives $21\le n\le19+\delta\le25$. The target is the large-order child; the argument below applies to all such two-connected graphs, independently of the extra local Ore witness.

Choose an ordinary longest path $P$, preferring one containing the unique vertex $z$ of degree below six if it exists. Let $J$ be the induced graph of its $m$ omitted vertices and call vertices of degree at least six high. All omitted high vertices have degree at least three in $J$. In addition, if an omitted low vertex has an omitted high neighbor, its ambient degree is at most twice its degree in $J$. Two disjoint links from an exterior $q$-cycle to $P$ give
$$|P|\ge 2q+\lceil q/2\rceil+3.$$
Indeed both exterior tails traverse the cycle and the interior interval uses its longer arc. The same tail and interval count applies to two distinct direct marks with distinct exterior contacts. [Two-link proof](../lowblock/Graph189TwoExteriorLinks.lean) and [exterior degree bound](../sep/cover/final82/lean/Graph189ExteriorDegree.lean)

If every cycle in $J$ has at most four vertices, the four-window rotation argument excludes every high omission. Its use of three-connectivity in the companion note is solely to exclude longer exterior cycles. The subsequent pivot count and two-tail count use only the ordinary longest path, $n\le25$ and the degree constraints. If no high vertex is omitted, the protected insertion theorem for $n\le22$ and the high-minimum gap for $n\ge23$ finish the proof. A cycle of length at least seven would already force $n\ge28$. [Four-window argument and insertion](degree-six-three-connected.md)

## A six-cycle

Here $|P|\ge18$ and hence $6\le m\le7$. If $m=6$, every high cycle vertex has at least one path contact. Consider two consecutive high vertices on the cycle. If their mark sets allow two distinct positions, the two tails and complementary cycle arc each use six vertices. This gives $|P|\ge20$ and $n\ge26$. Hence the two rows must be the same singleton. All high cycle vertices form a connected segment after deleting the possible low vertex, so they all see precisely one common mark. If the low vertex has any other mark, use either neighboring high vertex and the complementary arc to obtain the same contradiction. Thus the six-cycle is separated from the rest of $P$ by a single mark, contrary to two-connectivity.

The spanning six-cycle case passed the pinned kernel with the three standard axioms. [Six-cycle exclusion](../lowblock/Graph189SixCycleSpanning.lean)

For $m=7$, equality gives $n=25$, so $\delta=6$ and every vertex is high. The remaining exterior vertex $w$ has a neighbor $u$ on the cycle. Neither of the two cycle neighbors of $u$ sees $w$, since inserting $w$ at the corresponding edge would give a seven-cycle. These two forbidden neighbors show that $w$ has internal degree at most four and hence at least two path marks. One such cycle neighbor $y$ of $u$ misses $w$, so its internal degree is at most five and it also has a path mark. Choose different marks for $w$ and $y$. The edge from $w$ to $u$ followed by the complementary cycle arc from $u$ to $y$ spans all seven exterior vertices. This same path supplies both tails and the middle interval. Thus $|P|\ge23$, contrary to $n=25$.

## A five-cycle with additional high vertices

Write $C$ for the cycle vertices. There is no exterior cycle of length six or greater. Here $|P|\ge16$, so $5\le m\le9$. Every component $T$ of $J-C$ meets at most two cycle vertices. Two cycle neighbors of one vertex cannot be consecutive; otherwise the insertion gives a six-cycle. Also, if two different members of $T$ see two different cycle vertices, a path between the former inside $T$ and the longer arc of the five-cycle make an exterior cycle of at least six vertices. Thus an attached $T$ either has only one cycle anchor or just one member of $T$ touches $C$, in two nonconsecutive cycle vertices.

A component containing a high vertex has at least three vertices. If it had just one, its at most two cycle neighbors would violate its three required omitted neighbors. If it had exactly two, its high vertex would need two cycle anchors. Its companion would have no cycle anchor, since any of its anchors could be compared with a different one of the first vertex. With just the high companion as its omitted neighbor, its ambient degree would be at most two. Thus, as soon as there are at least two extra omissions, there are at least three, and $n\ge16+5+3=24$. The rooted order bound gives $\delta\ge5$. Every nonisolated omitted vertex now needs three omitted neighbors. This follows for a low vertex from any omitted neighbor, which is high. [Component lower bound](../lowblock/Graph189FiveCycleLinks.lean)

Any component meeting the cycle therefore has at least three vertices by the same argument. There are at most four vertices off the cycle and path, so all the components touching the cycle coincide. The set of their cycle anchors has size at most two. If every anchor is absent, the five cycle vertices form a set whose only external neighbors are on the path. The two-mark and layer-cover calculation below applies to this set and yields $n\le23$. [Anchor layout](../lowblock/Graph189FiveCycleComponent.lean) and [closed-set cover bound](../lowblock/Graph189FiveExteriorData.lean)

Otherwise take an anchor $c$, a neighbor $x$ of $c$ outside the cycle, and a neighbor $y$ of $x$ outside the cycle and path. Such a $y$ exists because $x$ needs three omitted neighbors and has at most two on the cycle. At least three cycle vertices have no neighbor off the path and cycle, and at least two of those are high. Each needs two path marks, so match two of these high vertices to distinct marks. Each begins a tail with the longer arc to $c$ (at least four cycle vertices) followed by $x,y$. The longer arc between the matched roots has at least four vertices. The tails, arc and two marks give $|P|\ge6+6+4+2=18$. Counting the at least eight omissions yields $n\ge26$, a contradiction. [Actual exterior tails](../lowblock/Graph189FiveCycleTails.lean)

## The low vertex lies outside the five-cycle

Here $m=6$ and all five cycle vertices are high. First suppose $z$ touches $C$. It has exactly two nonconsecutive neighbors there: one would give $\deg(z)\le2$ by the adjacent high neighbor bound, and three force a six-cycle. The same bound gives $\deg(z)\le4$ and so $n\le23$. Among the three cycle vertices not seeing $z$, two are consecutive on $C$. Both need at least two distinct path marks each because their internal degrees are at most four. Any matching of their two rows uses two full five-vertex tails and the full complementary five-vertex arc. Thus $|P|\ge17$, $n=23$ and, numbering from zero, the only two possible matched positions are $5,11$. Both rows must be exactly this pair. Every remaining cycle vertex needs at least one mark, since its internal degree is at most five. Applying the complementary-arc inequality on successive cycle edges propagates the conclusion that every cycle mark belongs to $\{5,11\}$.

At $n=23$ the rooted order bound gives $\delta\ge4$. Hence $z$ has degree four and has two path marks. A path from $z$ through a five-cycle neighbor can traverse all five cycle vertices, so every $z$-mark lies between indices $6$ and $10$. For either cycle neighbor $a$ of $z$, a route from $z$ to $a$ using its other neighbor and the longer cycle arc has at least five vertices. Thus marks of $z$ and $a$ at different positions must differ by at least six. Neither $5$ nor $11$ is that far from any index $6,\ldots,10$, a contradiction.

If $z$ is isolated in $J$, all five cycle rows have at least two marks. Again two consecutive rows give $|P|\ge17$ and $n\ge23$, whence $\delta\ge4$. All neighbors of $z$ are on the path. Without a pair of $z$-marks two apart, their predecessors and successors give at least eight distinct vertices at distance two from $z$. Swapping $z$ for the midpoint of such a pair makes another ordinary longest path containing $z$, contradicting the choice of $P$.

## Exactly five omitted vertices

The five-cycle has a five-vertex tail beginning at any root and a four-vertex path between every pair of distinct prescribed roots by the longer arc. A matching of three marks would therefore give $n\ge5+3+5+5+4+4=26$. At least four cycle vertices are high and each needs two marks. The checked matching lemma consequently gives the same two marks on every high row, and covers all remaining rows. [Three-mark estimate and row theorem](../lowblock/Graph189FiveCycleContacts.lean)

Consequently exactly two marks meet the cycle, every high cycle member sees both, and all contacts of the possible low vertex lie among them. Every high vertex must also have all four internal neighbors to reach six. The five omissions form a clique.

At any high vertex $x$ of this clique the two marks cover the entire second layer. The other neighbors of $x$ are the four other omitted vertices and these have no other path contact. The checked rooted cover estimate gives $n\le 1+6+6+2(7-2)=23$. Both marks and the three gaps between or beyond them together occupy at most eighteen vertices. Every gap has at least five, so at least two gaps have exactly five. If $z$ was on $P$, choose such a gap avoiding $z$. If $z$ was omitted, use the whole clique in the exchange and thus put $z$ on the new path. Exchange the chosen gap for a spanning path through the clique, using two different high contacts for an interior gap. This gives another ordinary longest path and all five newly omitted vertices are high.

On the new path, internal minimum degree of its five omissions is at least three, so they are Hamilton-connected by prescribed-endpoint closure. The preceding matching and cover argument therefore makes all of their contact rows identical. A bounding old mark, which is still on the new path and sees an endpoint of the exchanged gap, sees all five newly omitted vertices. Take any high vertex $x$ of the old omitted clique. It was nonadjacent to all five of these old gap vertices, so they are now known to lie at distance two from $x$ in the original graph. The four shifts of the two original marks are distinct, and at most two lie in the exchanged gap. At least seven vertices are therefore at distance two from $x$, contradicting the cap of six. The pinned kernel checked the resulting contradiction through the full old-path and exchanged-path hypotheses.
