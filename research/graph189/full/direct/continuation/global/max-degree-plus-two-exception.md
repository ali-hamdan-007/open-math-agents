# A maximum degree at most two above the second-smallest degree

Let G satisfy the original Graph 189 assumptions, with second-smallest degree k and Δ≤k+2. Then G has a Hamiltonian path, with no restriction on k, order or vertex connectivity. The least degree may be arbitrarily far below k. The established k≤5 theorem, cut-vertex theorem, and the full two-cut theorems in ../sep/unrestricted-two-cuts.md and ../sep/small-two-cuts.md leave only κ(G)≥3, k≥6.

We give the changes to the full proof in three-consecutive-degrees-all.md, retaining all notation. Work at k≥6, κ≥3 and assume nontraceability. The rooted version of the refined wedge theorem, rooted at a vertex of minimum degree, again gives n≤5k−3. Let z be the unique low vertex, if any. Choose P with the square-maximizing rule and C the smallest high-bearing exterior component. Such C exists by the almost-spanning theorem, since otherwise only a single low vertex is omitted. For every high x∈C, d_C(x)≥h=ceil(k/2), and |C|≥h+2.

For a noncut high x, every two-step mark overlap has a low midpoint: the same exchange contradicts square maximality for any high midpoint, which must touch some other high-bearing component of order at least |C| (at least h≥3 other exterior neighbors are required after the exchange, and there is at most one low singleton). Thus r=|D₂(x)∩C|≤2q−k+ε where q=d_C(x), and ε=1 only if the possible low vertex lies on P; otherwise ε=0. For every high neighbor y of x, the a_x+a_y distinct path witnesses imply d_X(y)≥k−q in X=N_C[x]; this also holds at x. Every high member of X has ≥k−q−r contacts.

## Endblocks with a low vertex anywhere

If C is wholly high and has a cut vertex, choose a smallest endblock as in the companion. Its argument uses only high vertices within C and so applies regardless of the low vertex's location outside C.

If z∈C and C has a cut vertex, choose an endblock B all of whose noncut vertices are high, and let w be its articulation, b=|B| and q the least noncut degree within B. Write R'=C−B. It contains a high vertex: otherwise R' is the singleton low z and its only C-neighbor is the high w. The path contact inequality a_z≤1+c_C(z,w) then gives d(z)≤2, contrary to κ≥3. Thus |R'|≥h as any high member loses at most w from its C-neighborhood.

If b≥2q−1, three connectors to the Fan cycle in B give n≥8q+h+2. For k=6,7,8,9 this already exceeds 5k−3 at q≥ceil(k/2). For k≥10 it gives a=2k−3q≥(k+3h+15)/8≥5. For a minimizing noncut high x, delete the possible low member of X. The high subgraph has size ≥q, minimum degree ≥k−q−1, and is Hamilton-connected: its order is q+1−e with e=0 or 1, and 3q≤2k−5. Its high members have ≥a contacts, and a≤q. Therefore n≥b+h+a+(a+1)q≥(a+3)q+h+a−1. At a≥6 this exceeds 5k−3 since q,h≥k/2. At a=5 substitute q=(2k−5)/3: the excess is k/3+h−19/3>0 when k≥10.

If b≤2q−2, B−w is Hamilton-connected. The t=max(2,k−b+1) matches from its members exist as in the companion. Each tail traverses B to w and continues into R' through at least h−1 vertices: take a shortest route from w to any high R' member and then a rooted path of ≥h−1 high vertices in that member's high induced R'-component, where degrees have lost at most w and z. A shortest route to the first high vertex uses only z as a possible intermediate. Thus n≥(t+2)b+3h−1. On b≥k−1 the lower bound exceeds 5k−3 since 3h>k+2. On [h+1,k−2], both concavity endpoint bounds from the companion also exceed 5k−3 after the loss of two: the lower-end margins are (h−2)² or h²−5h+8, positive at all relevant h; the upper-end margin is 3h−8>0. This proves C must be two-connected.

## The near-Hamilton-connected exterior

Take a high x of minimum C-degree q among high members; Fan gives circumference ≥min(m,2q), m=|C|. Suppose m≥2q. Then n≥8q+5, q≤(5k−8)/8≤k−3. Put X=N_C[x], R=D₂(x)∩C, and I=C−(X∪R).

If z∈C, we have r=|R|≤2q−k≤q−3. Thus |I|≥2 and it contains a high member. The high subgraph H on X after deleting the possible low vertex has ≥q members, minimum degree ≥k−q−1 and is Hamilton-connected as above since a=2k−3q≥(k+24)/8>3. Match a of its high members to P. For a prescribed H port v, take a path in C−v from H−v to a high member of I. Trim it at the last H vertex w' before its first high I member y. Its interior contains an R vertex since there is no X–I edge, and no high I vertex occurs before y. Traverse H from v to w', follow the connector, then continue inside high I from y for ≥q−r vertices because that high graph has minimum degree ≥q−r−1. Hence each tail routed through H gains at least k−q+1. Consequently n≥2q+a+(a+1)q+2(k−q+1)=(a+1)q+a+2k+2. For a≥5 this exceeds 5k−3 via q≥k/2. For a=4 use q=(2k−4)/3 to obtain the same contradiction.

If C is wholly high, r≤2q−k+1≤q−2. With the weaker a=2k−3q−1≥(k+16)/8 we again match a in X, now of size q+1. It is Hamilton-connected by 2(k−q)≥q+2. There is an I vertex, and all I members have ≥q−r neighbors there. The companion's two tail extensions gain ≥q−r+2≥k−q+1 apiece. Thus n≥(a+1)q+2a+2k+3. For a≥5 this is immediate, and for a=4 use q=(2k−5)/3. The only possibility a≤3 has k≤8; at k=6,7 the preceding inequality 8q+5≤5k−3 is impossible, and at k=8 it forces q=4,a=3, giving n≥41>37. This excludes m≥2q in all cases.

Thus m≤2q−1. There is a spanning cycle in C by Fan. Further, any two prescribed ports admit a spanning path, except possibly the pair u,v when z∈C has exactly its two neighbors u,v in C. Indeed the degree-two endpoint-marker closure completes all high vertices (their degrees at least q give degree sum ≥m+1); the remaining exceptional vertex has two or more neighbors, with only this one pair possibly obstructed. For the exceptional pair there is always a path on m−1 vertices from u to v: delete z and add a marker adjacent to u and v, restoring exactly the degrees these two lost. All m−1 original vertices have degree ≥q, so closure on this m-vertex graph makes them complete. Reversing closure and removing the marker gives the claimed path.

## Resolving the exceptional contact pair

For t disjoint matched C–P contacts we consequently have n≥(t+2)m+t−1: both tails span C, and at most one consecutive pair of distinct matched C endpoints can be exceptional, at a cost of one. By κ≥3 there are three matches, giving m≤k−1. Equality m=k−1 would require an exceptional pair u,v. The vertex z then has a P contact (its C-degree is two and κ≥3). Choose a high C vertex y besides z,u,v, possible since m≥h+2. It misses z in C and has ≥k−(m−2)=3 contacts. Match y and z at distinct marks, and extend these two matched C vertices to a three-matching: augmenting paths preserve coverage of already matched C vertices. At most one of u,v can occur in the three, so both gaps span C, giving n≥5m+3=5k−2, impossible.

We have m≤k−2 and put a=k−m+1≥3, with k≥2a+2 from m≥h+2. There are ≥a high C vertices because m−1≥h+1≥a. Each has ≥a contacts. A matching of a+1 would give n≥(a+3)m+a (one less than in the companion), still exceeding 5k−3 by at least a²−3a+2>0. Thus the matching number is exactly a and a minimum cover of this size must consist entirely of marks A: otherwise an uncovered high member has ≥a contacts off a set of ≤a−1 marks. Every high C member touches all A, and the cover shows all contacts of C lie in A. Each high C member must attain all m−1 internal neighbors to reach k. Thus C is a clique including the possible z; only the low member may miss some marks.

All marks have high C neighbors, so each gap and tail has ≥m unmarked vertices and they retain this property under every same-vertex rotation. A high attainable endpoint e avoiding A exists. If P is wholly high, the k+1 endpoint count in the companion suffices by looking at any high x∈C. If z∈P, the clique C is wholly high with identical contact set and the earlier arbitrary-low high-endpoint lemma in common-attachment-clique.md applies. Equivalently one can invoke the proved |R|≥k+2 endpoint bound, leaving ≥k+1 high endpoints against ≤k even witnesses for x. Therefore σ≥a exactly as in the companion, and a≥4 is excluded by the same arithmetic. We have a=3, m=k−2, k≥8, n≥5k−4 and |P|≥4k−2.

If the low z∈C has no contact, partition V−(A∪C) into X₁,Y,Z according to distance 1,2,≥3 from A. Every Y member is at distance four from z as the only exits use a high C member followed by a mark. So |Y|≤k. From any high x∈C, X₁ and Z are positive even witnesses: Z is outside C and high and at distance exactly four. Hence |V−C|≤3+2k, impossible.

Otherwise some mark touches all of C. Choose a fixed high x∈C. Orient P so that every successor of its three marks is high. This is automatic unless z∈P, and even there the marks have ≥m unmarked vertices in every gap; a low vertex cannot be both a predecessor and a successor of marks. The rooted defect-sum bound and the **refined** wedge inequality give

    Σ(d(successor)−k) ≤ 3(5k−3−n)/(k−2) ≤ 3/(k−2)<1.

Indeed sum the refined wedge bound over the three successor wedges, use the rooted sum of defects ≥3, d(x)≥k, d(mark)≤k+2 and e(mark)≤k. Thus every successor has degree k. Choose w the successor of the mark touching all of C, so all k−2 members are in D₂(w). Only two other vertices can be at positive even distance. Any fourth layer from w has size ≤2 and cannot separate a fifth layer in a 3-connected graph. Now the same penultimate count as in the companion gives |V−C|≤3k+5<4k−2: at most two outside penultimates serve ≤k targets apiece, and penultimates in C reach only the marks. This contradiction completes the κ≥3 proof.

The external dependencies agree with the companion plus the all-order almost-spanning result in ../lowblock/all-near-spanning.md, the one-low high-endpoint lemma from the earlier rotation checkpoint, and the full connectivity-two result in ../sep/unrestricted-two-cuts.md and ../sep/small-two-cuts.md. The graph proof here and the combined two-cut theorem received independent paper audits. This theorem has no end-to-end Lean formalization.
