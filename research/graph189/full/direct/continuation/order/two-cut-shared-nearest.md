# Two deep components at a separating pair

Let $k$ be the second-smallest degree of a qualifying graph and let $S$ be a separating pair. The graph has vertex connectivity at least two. A port is a vertex of a side adjacent to a member of $S$. A high vertex has degree at least $k$. Each side is nonempty and no edge joins the sides.

## Both sides contain a high nonport with one nearest anchor

A pair of opposite high nonports has distance four. A shortest path crosses $S$ at distance two from each endpoint. In particular, if both vertices have a unique anchor at distance two, the two anchors coincide. Denote it by $i$.

Consider a high nonport $x$ on one side with sole nearest anchor $i$. Its two-edge path to $i$ has a predecessor adjacent to $i$ and to no other anchor. This predecessor sees every opposite $i$-contact at distance two. The separator boundary costs it one further even vertex. The opposite side therefore has at most $k-1$ such contacts. Every opposite high vertex missing $i$ is at even distance from $x$. Otherwise the two high vertices would have distance three, and crossing the separator on a shortest path would require a contact at the sole nearest anchor. The nonport spends two even vertices outside the opposite side, leaving at most $k-2$ high noncontacts. These bounds apply in both directions. [Sole-nearest budgets](Graph189SoleNearestData.lean)

An all-high side has size at most $2k-3$, internal minimum degree at least $k-2$, and at most $k-2$ vertices missing $i$. A Hamilton path in that side can be rotated and trimmed to start at an $i$-contact while losing at most one vertex. [Dense side routes](Graph189DenseSideRoute.lean)

If one side contains the low vertex $z$, erase it. The remaining region $R$ has internal minimum degree at least $d=k-3$, order at most $2k-3$ and at most $k-2$ noncontacts. Its high nonport from the hypothesis has $k$ neighbors in the original side, so $R$ has at least $k$ vertices and contains an $i$-contact. If $R$ is connected or has order at most $2d+1$, its longest path misses at most two vertices. Rotating and trimming to an $i$-contact costs at most two more. Including $z$, the side loses at most five.

In the other case, $R$ is disconnected and has more than $2d+1$ vertices. It contains at least three $i$-contacts. In the original connected side each vertex other than $z$ has degree at least $d+1$, and the order is at most $3d$. Split the components after deleting $z$ into two nonempty groups. Each group has at least $d+1$ vertices and minimum internal degree at least $d$, so both have order at most $2d-1$. Both induced graphs are Hamilton-connected by the prescribed-endpoint Ore bound. A group with at least two marked contacts can start at one distinct from a chosen neighbor of $z$. Joining through $z$ to a path spanning the other group gives a spanning side path beginning at a mark. [Marked articulation](Graph189MarkedLowCut.lean)

Join the two side paths through $i$. The losses on the two sides are at most five and one, and at most one anchor is missed. The two side bounds and the unique low vertex give $n\le4k-3$. In that range a path omitting at most $k-3$ vertices forces traceability for $k\ge10$. For a hypothetical Hamiltonian deletion of a low vertex of degree three use the low-deletion theorem. For degree at least four the rooted index-four bound gives $n+1\ge5(k-2)$, contradicting the order bound. Minimum degree two is already solved. If a longest path omits $m\le k-3$ vertices, the ordinary exterior inequalities now give $6\le m$ and $m(k+3-m)\le n+2$. But $m(k+3-m)-6(k-3)=(m-6)(k-3-m)\ge0$ and $6(k-3)>4k-1\ge n+2$. Our seven omissions suffice for $k\ge10$. [Linear-order path gap](Graph189PairLinearGap.lean) If a chosen side was disconnected already, deleting the separating pair gives at least three components, which the three-component theorem excludes in this range. [Two-cut assembly](Graph189SeparatedSideComponents.lean)

## Both deep sides have a common port

Assume each side contains a high nonport and a vertex adjacent to both anchors. Either common port sees all opposite ports at distance two. Each side therefore has at most $k$ ports. Each side has at most $k-2$ high nonports, by applying the separator debit to a high nonport in the other side, which sees them all at distance four.

If the sole-nearest case does not apply, there is a side $A$ in which every high nonport is at distance two from both anchors. Write $r$ for their number and $a,b$ for $k$ minus the two side port counts. The two port sets, the two even-distance sets at the anchors, and the possible low vertex cover the graph. Every high nonport is counted at least once and those in $A$ are counted twice. There is one more double count. If the anchors are nonadjacent, their common neighbor puts the second anchor in both even rows. If they are adjacent, take a high nonport $y$ in $B$ and a two-edge route $iwy$ from a nearest anchor. The middle vertex $w$ is a $B$-port. Write $j$ for the other anchor. An edge $jw$ puts $y$ in both even rows. Without that edge $w$ lies in the even row of $j$ and the $B$-port set. In every case $n+r+1\le4k+3-a-b$. Counting the two sides separately gives $n\le3k+r+1-a-b$. If $a+b\ge2$ then $2n+1\le7k$, so $n+5<4k$ for $k\ge10$. If $a+b\le1$, both common ports see at least $k-1$ opposite ports. The second-layer argument below shows that all but one nonport per side are adjacent to their common port. A single anchor consequently sees all but two nonports in the entire graph at distance two. Thus $n\le3k+4$. The below-four-times-degree theorem finishes both alternatives at $k\ge10$, and the sole-nearest result covers the other branch already at $k\ge10$. The two anchors may be adjacent or nonadjacent. ## Common ports with a shallow side

Suppose instead that all high vertices of side $D$ are ports. If $D$ has no high vertex, it is a singleton of degree two, covered by the original degree-two theorem. Otherwise let $b$ be its number of ports. The opposite common port sees them all at distance two, so $b\le k$. The side has at most one further vertex. A high vertex in $D$ can have at most two neighbors in the separator, giving $|D|\ge k-1$ and $b\ge k-2$.

If $b\ge k-1$, any common port $v$ of the opposite side $C$ has at most one distance-two vertex in $C$. These vertices separate $v$ from any farther vertex of $C$. Indeed a neighbor of a farther vertex lies in $C$ or in the separator, and each anchor is adjacent to $v$. Any first step out of the vertices at distance greater than two must therefore land in $C$ at distance two. Two-connectivity rules out a farther vertex. Consequently all but at most one $C$ nonport neighbor $v$ and are at distance two from either anchor. There are at most $k+1$ nonports and at most $k$ ports in $C$, giving $n\le3k+4$.

If $b=k-2$, then $|D|=k-1$, with a unique low nonport $z$. A high $x\in D$ has $k$ neighbors and hence touches every other vertex of $D$ and both anchors. Every opposite vertex is exactly one step farther from $z$ than from $x$. To see the lower bound, any route from $z$ crosses an anchor after at least two steps, and $x$ can reach that anchor in one step. The edge $zx$ gives the upper bound. The even budgets of $x,z$ thus cover $C$, so $|C|\le2k$. This again gives $n\le3k+4$. The original below-four-times-degree bound finishes the shallow branch at $k\ge10$. ## Adjacent anchors with a common-free side

Let $p,q$ be adjacent. At a vertex outside the even-distance sets of both anchors their distances are odd, and the triangle inequality forces them to be equal. Suppose $A$ has no common port. If its opposite side $B$ contains a high nonport, every high vertex of $A$ lies within distance two of an anchor. A high odd tie in $A$ would therefore be a common port, so $A$ contains at most one odd tie. More generally, suppose there is a high odd tie $x$ in $A$. Its two depths coincide at an odd number at least three. Every port in $B$ lies exactly one step farther from $x$ than either anchor, hence at even distance. The nonport separator debit leaves at most $k-2$ such $B$-ports. Every high $B$ vertex is a port, since opposite high nonports would put $x$ at distance two from an anchor. If $B$ has no high vertex it is a degree-two singleton. Otherwise the borderline shallow argument applies on $B$ with at most $k-2$ ports. It gives $n\le3k+1$ using the parity rows of the low nonport and any high $B$ vertex.

If $A$ has no high odd tie, choose a private $p$-contact in $A$, which exists by minimality of the separating pair. Its distance to every odd tie in $B$ is the common odd depth plus one. It also sees $q$ at distance two. Hence $B$ contains at most $k-1$ odd ties. Adding the two inclusive anchor budgets gives $n\le3k+2$. Both bounds give $n+5<4k$ at $k\ge9$. The low-anchor argument at this threshold uses the verified tight-pair theorem. Every low anchor has degree $k-1$, with a genuine neighbor in each side. The tight-pair paths close that branch, so completing a low vertex of any remaining counterexample raises connectivity to three. Together with the both-common case, this proves the original conclusion for **every adjacent separating pair at $k\ge10$** on paper. ## Balanced components of order at most $2k-1$

For any simple path whose last vertex is adjacent to its first, a prescribed vertex $z$ in the same connected graph starts a path containing at least as many vertices. Choose a vertex $v$ of the closed path nearest to $z$, take a shortest $z,v$ path, and rotate the closed path to open it at $v$. Minimality of the distance ensures the two paths meet only at $v$. This also covers a closed walk obtained from a single path edge and its reverse. [Cycle entry](Graph189MarkedCycleEntry.lean)

Let $M$ be a nonempty set of marked vertices in a connected graph, and consider a longest path. If either endpoint belongs to $M$, orient it from that endpoint. Otherwise let $u$ bound below both endpoint degrees. Suppose $n<2u+|M|$. The two sets of predecessor pivots obtainable by one rotation have sizes at least $u$. If either meets $M$, that rotation preserves the whole path and starts at a mark. Otherwise the pivot sets intersect. As in the two-sided rotation proof, their common pivot opens a cycle containing all path vertices except this pivot. The cycle-entry result supplies a path from a mark, even if every mark lies off that cycle. Thus passing to a marked start costs at most one vertex beyond the ordinary longest-path deficit. [Connected marked route](Graph189ConnectedMarkedRoute.lean)

Consider now a separating pair whose two sides have at most $2k-1$ vertices each, with $k\ge12$. The adjacent and three-component cases are already covered, so take connected sides and nonadjacent anchors. We may assume $n\ge4k-5$, hence both sides have at least $2k-6$ vertices and every edge belongs to a triangle. Each anchor has at least two contacts in either side: a contact edge's common neighbor cannot be the other, nonadjacent anchor.

Each side has at least four distinct ports. This holds in a common-free side because its two contact sets are disjoint. If $A$ contains a common port, it sees all opposite ports at distance two, so $B$ has at most $k$ ports. Since $|B|\ge2k-6$, it has a high nonport. That vertex sees all high $A$ nonports at distance four, with the separator debit leaving at most $k-2$ such vertices. At most one low vertex remains, so $A$ has at least $|A|-k+1\ge k-5$ ports. This proves the four-port claim regardless of the common ports on either side.

A wholly high side has internal minimum degree at least $k-2$, and its ordinary longest path misses at most two vertices. For any selected anchor, take its contacts as marks. Unmarked endpoints have internal degree at least $k-1$. The two contacts therefore give the room inequality above, leaving a marked path missing at most three vertices.

For the other side, erase its possible low vertex $z$. If all vertices there are high, an arbitrary vertex can play the same role. The surviving internal minimum degree is at least $k-3$. Mark every surviving port, of which there are at least three. If the deletion disconnects, the marked-articulation theorem gives a spanning path beginning at a port. Otherwise its longest path misses at most three vertices, and an unmarked endpoint retains at least $k-1$ neighbors. The same rotation loses one more vertex. Counting $z$, the resulting side path omits at most five vertices. It can use either anchor. The wholly high side matches that selected anchor, leaving at most nine omitted vertices in the entire graph. Here $n\le4k$ and $k-3\ge9$, so the verified linear-order omission theorem forces a spanning path. These arguments give the balanced-pair conclusion without any hypotheses about where the low vertex lies or how the ports are distributed. ## A shallow common side with both private types

Let $A$ be shallow among high vertices and contain a common port and private ports of both anchor types. Suppose its opposite side $B$ is common-free. For $k\ge12$, adjacent anchors and disconnected choices of a side are covered by the preceding results. Take nonadjacent anchors and connected $A$. Each member of $S$ has a private contact in $B$. Its even row and the separator debit bound the matching anchor contacts in $A$ by $k-1$. Thus $a+c\le2k-1$, where $a=|A|$ and $c$ counts common ports in $A$. This counts a possible low nonport once. In particular $a\le2k-1$, so $b\le2k-1$ is the balanced case.

Assume $b\ge2k$. Every high private in $A$ must be at distance two from its missing anchor. Otherwise the far-private-port estimate would give $b\le2k-1$, since the opposite private type supplies an $A$-vertex missing the first anchor. For any private type having only a low representative $z$, choose a neighbor in connected $A$. This neighbor is high; it touches the missing anchor because no high private of the original type exists. Thus both private types supply a representative with distances $(1,2)$ or $(2,1)$. At least one is high, so $a\ge k$. The high-pair bound and separator debit now show that every $B$ vertex at distance at least three from both anchors is low. Write $w$ for the number at distance two from both. The private representative rows give $b+2\le2k+w+1$. Together with $a+c\le2k-1$ this proves $n+c\le4k+w$.

The two inclusive anchor rows, the $c$ common $A$ ports, all $B$ ports and the possible low vertex cover the graph. Every high $A$ private is in an anchor row, and each high $B$ nonport is in at least one. The $B$ ports number at most $k$ by the common $A$ contact. The rows overlap at the $w$ double-near vertices and at both anchors: their mutual distance is two via the common port. Consequently $n+w\le3k+c+1$, giving $2n\le7k+1$. This contradicts $n\ge4k-5$ for $k\ge12$. [Shallow mixed-pair implication](Graph189ShallowMixedPair.lean)

## A shallow side missing a private type

Suppose $B$ has no common port and $A$ has no $q$-private port. Every port in $A$ touches $p$. The $p$-contact in $B$ is private, so it bounds the number of $A$-contacts of $p$ by $k-1$. If there are at most $k-2$, the shallow parity argument gives $n\le3k+1$. With exactly $k-1$, all high vertices in $A$ touch $p$ and $k-1\le a\le k$. If $b\le2k+1$, this gives $n\le3k+3$, enough for $k\ge9$.

In the remaining case $b>2k+1$, so $a<b$. The selector takes a globally smallest side among all separating pairs. If the selected pair was presented in the opposite orientation, its minimality would require $b\le a$. Thus this $A$ has the global minimum required by the gate argument. Connected $B$, the triangle on each edge at $n>3k+2$, and the exact $k-1$ contacts give $n\le3k+2$ by the checked graph-facing recut theorem.
