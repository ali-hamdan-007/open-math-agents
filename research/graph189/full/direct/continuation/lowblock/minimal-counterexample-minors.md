# Small deletions and contractions in a minimum-order counterexample

Let G be a counterexample to Graph 189 minimizing first its order and then its number of edges, with n vertices, k its second-smallest degree, h=ceil(k/2). The global almost-spanning theorem, the earlier unrooted exterior bound, and the known low-degree and cut-vertex cases show that every path in G uses at most n−h−2 vertices. Indeed a longest path missing ≤h+1 vertices could omit only the unique low singleton, which the almost-spanning theorem excludes.

Consequently every connected induced subgraph G−S with 1≤|S|≤h+1 fails the Graph 189 condition: a spanning path in it would be a sufficiently long G path. More generally, every proper connected minor H of G on at least n−h−1 vertices fails the condition. A spanning path in H always lifts to a path in G using at least |V(H)| vertices. Fix one representative original edge for every consecutive pair of branch sets on that path. Inside each connected branch set take a simple path between its two chosen incident ends, or the singleton if they coincide. Use a single chosen end in a terminal branch set. Concatenation gives a simple path since the branch sets are disjoint. If H qualifies, its smaller order (or same order and fewer edges) forces it to have a spanning path, a contradiction. This allows arbitrary simultaneous connected contractions, vertex deletions and edge deletions, subject only to the lower bound on the minor order. For a single deletion or contraction the choice of G among graphs of equal order is immaterial.

For isometric deletion of a single vertex v, let t_v be the number of remaining vertices x satisfying deg_G(x)−1_{x~v}<k. Distances on G−v are preserved if and only if every nonadjacent pair in N(v) has a second common neighbor (replace any shortest path segment through v). This includes a vertex whose closed neighborhood is contained in that of a neighbor. In this setting t_v≤1 implies G−v qualifies. If t_v≥2, its second-smallest degree is k−1 as long as at most one original vertex has degree below k. Then G−v qualifies exactly if every remaining vertex attaining the full even cap sees v at even distance. Hence in any minimal counterexample every isometrically deletable v must have ≥2 subthreshold survivors and some saturated remaining vertex at odd distance from v. This extends the earlier true-twin criterion to all isometrically deletable vertices using the global almost-spanning theorem. In particular if z is the unique low vertex, isometrically removable, at least two neighbors of z must have degree exactly k. No lifting property special to twins is required.

## The minimum-degree vertex can always be deleted isometrically

Assume n>3k+2. If deg(v)≤k and a,b are nonadjacent high neighbors of v then they have a common neighbor other than v. Indeed their neighborhoods both lie in B₂(v)∖{a,b}, so

    |N(a)∩N(b)|≥2k−(deg(v)+k−1)=k−deg(v)+1.

This suffices when deg(v)<k. If deg(v)=k and v is the only common neighbor, every bound is tight: deg(a)=deg(b)=k, the exact second layer at v has k vertices, and N[a]∪N[b]=B₂(v). Consequently there is no fourth layer (the even budget at v is exhausted) or any later layer. Write E(w) for all even-distance vertices from w, now including w itself. Then E(v) covers v and its second layer. Any other neighbor of v besides a,b sees exactly one of a,b by an edge, hence sees the other at distance two through v. Each third-layer vertex touches a member of the second layer, which itself touches a or b, so belongs to E(a)∪E(b). Since a and b are both in E(a)∩E(b), these three sets give n≤3(k+1)−2=3k+1, a contradiction.

In particular every minimum-degree vertex is isometrically deletable, since its neighbors are all high whether or not this minimum is attained more than once. When the minimum is unique, at least two neighbors of the low vertex must therefore attain degree exactly k in a minimum counterexample. When the minimum is tied, the graph induced by degree-k vertices has minimum degree at least two and thus contains a cycle: apply the same deletion criterion to each such vertex. In the unique-low case any degree-k vertex nonadjacent to the low vertex is likewise isometrically deletable, so must be adjacent to at least one degree-k vertex (the original low vertex survives). If a degree-k vertex v adjacent to the low z fails isometric deletability, the offending pair must consist of z and a nonneighbor of z whose sole common neighbor with z is v.

## A simultaneous deletion forcing a common saturated odd witness

Suppose u has minimum degree, v is a degree-k neighbor, and at most one common neighbor of u,v has degree exactly k. Then G−{u,v} is isometric and its second-smallest degree is at least k−1. In a minimum counterexample there is therefore a saturated surviving vertex at the same odd distance from u and v, necessarily one or three.

Indeed G' = G−u is isometric, v has degree k−1 there, and G' has the old even cap k. Among two nonadjacent neighbors a,b of v in G', at most one could have degree below k there, by the common-neighbor hypothesis (the only original possible low was u). Each has degree at least k−1 and their degree sum is at least 2k−1. Their neighborhoods lie in B₂(v)∖{a,b}, of size at most 2k−2. If v is their sole common neighbor, then every inequality is tight: the exact second v-layer fully consumes k, and B₂(v)=N[a]∪N[b]. Applying the same three even-set covering argument inside G' gives |G'|≤3k+1, contrary to n−1≥3k+2. We can thus delete v isometrically from G'. The only possible degree below k−1 in G−{u,v} is a common degree-k neighbor, of which there is at most one. If every surviving saturated vertex had even distance to u or v, each survivor would retain at most k−1 even witnesses, making the double deletion qualifying. Any obstructing vertex is at odd distance from both vertices; adjacency forces the distances to agree. The distance-five endpoint theorem rules out five since the survivor and v are both high. In particular every edge of a triangle-free component of the tied-minimum degree-k subgraph has a common saturated odd witness.

## Arbitrary separated sets of isometrically removable vertices

Let S be any nonempty collection of individually isometrically deletable vertices at pairwise distance at least three. Then G−S is isometric. Indeed every deleted vertex's entire neighborhood and every second-common-neighbor detour for a nonadjacent pair of its neighbors survive all the other deletions: such a common vertex lies within distance two of that center. Thus the local detour criterion still holds after each of the other deletions. Every survivor loses at most one neighbor. In particular the second-smallest degree in G−S is at least k−1. All such sets automatically satisfy |S|≤h+1: their disjoint closed neighborhoods give

    δ+1+(|S|−1)(k+1)≤n≤1+δ+k+floor((k+1)²/4),

and (h+1)(k+1)>k+floor((k+1)²/4). Therefore some surviving saturated vertex must have odd distance to every member of S; otherwise the connected minor G−S qualifies. This simultaneously applies to every separated set of degree-k vertices when the minimum is tied.

There is a larger band of removable vertices in that tied case. Set q=ceil((n−3k−1)/k)>0. For any nonisometric vertex v choose nonadjacent neighbors a,b with v their sole common neighbor. The set N(v)∖(N(a)∪N(b)) contains a,b, so j=|N(a)∪N(v)∪N(b)|−d(a)−d(b)≥1. By the separately written local Ore inequality (verify/wedge-order-and-hamiltonicity.md), d(v)+e(v)−d(a)−d(b)≥q. Thus every vertex v with d(v)+e(v)<2k+q, including every vertex of degree <k+q, is isometrically removable. In a minimum tied counterexample every such vertex must have at least two degree-k neighbors and every separated collection of them has the single saturated odd witness above.

## Exact contraction metric and a sufficient test in diameter at most four

Fix an edge uv. Vertices other than u,v are split into U,V,E according as they are closer to u, closer to v, or equidistant. Distances to the contracted vertex w are minima of the two original distances. For survivors x,y outside w, the distance can decrease by exactly one only when some shortest x–y path uses uv, and in that case it does decrease by one. This follows by contracting such a shortest path in one direction, and lifting a projected shortest path with at most one inserted edge in the other direction. An old odd distance can change to even in diameter ≤4 only at distance three. For survivors x,y this happens precisely when x∈N(u)∖N[v], y∈N(v)∖N[u] (or reversed) and d_G(x,y)=3. In particular a survivor with such a gain was adjacent to exactly one endpoint and stood at distance two from the other: identifying the endpoints already removes one of its even witnesses. Thus if the cross-pairs at distance three between the two private-neighbor sets form a matching (possibly empty), no surviving vertex outside w increases its even count.

For the merged vertex itself let U_even,U_odd record the numbers in U of even or odd distances from u, and V_even,V_odd the corresponding counts from v. Using positive even counts e_G and excluding u,v from U,V,E, direct partition gives

    e_{G/uv}(w)−e_G(u)=V_even−V_odd,
    e_{G/uv}(w)−e_G(v)=U_even−U_odd.

In particular at least one weakly odd-majority private side suffices to keep w below the cap. Remaining degrees drop only at common neighbors of u,v (by one), and w has degree |N(u)∪N(v)|−2. Therefore any edge satisfying the cross-matching condition, at least one weak odd-majority side, and at most one surviving subthreshold degree (including w), contracts to a qualifying graph. A minimum counterexample of diameter at most four cannot contain such an edge. If u is the unique low vertex and exactly one vertex of G has degree exactly k, the degree condition is automatic for every edge at u: all survivors except possibly that degree-k vertex and w have degree at least k, and if the degree-k vertex survives, w has degree at least deg(v)−1≥k.
