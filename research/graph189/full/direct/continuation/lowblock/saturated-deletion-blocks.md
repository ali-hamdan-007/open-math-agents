# A saturated vertex of large degree or a triangle with two saturated vertices

Let G be a minimum-order counterexample to Graph 189 with minimum degree δ and second-smallest degree k. Write n for its order, h=ceil(k/2), and q=ceil((n−3k−1)/k). A vertex is saturated if it has k other vertices at positive even distance. Write b(v)=k unless v neighbors the unique vertex of degree δ<k, when b(v)=δ. At least one of the following must hold:

1. A saturated vertex has degree at least b(v)+q.
2. There is a triangle with two saturated vertices whose third vertex is saturated or has degree at most k.

When the minimum degree is tied, b(v)=k everywhere. In that case all saturated vertices cannot be confined to a triangle-free subgraph induced by the degree-k vertices. We use the previously established n>3k+2, k≥6, the bound n≤1+δ+k+floor((k+1)²/4), and the fact that every path in a counterexample misses at least h+2 vertices. The local Ore wedge estimate and the isometric single-deletion criteria are in verify/wedge-order-and-hamiltonicity.md and lowblock/minimal-counterexample-minors.md.

## Selecting the vertices to delete

Suppose neither outcome holds. Starting with all saturated vertices, while the remaining pool contains an edge, select its two endpoints as a block and remove from the pool every point at distance at most two from either. Any removed point other than the endpoints is at positive even distance from at least one endpoint. Otherwise the two distances are both odd. One is at most two and therefore one, and adjacency between the selected endpoints forces the other also to be one. That gives a triangle of three saturated vertices. Once the pool is independent, select any remaining singleton and remove the points at distance at most two from it, repeating to exhaustion. For singletons, unselected points removed from the independent pool are at distance two. The resulting blocks are mutually at distance at least three, and every surviving saturated vertex is at even distance from a selected vertex. Denote the set of all selected vertices by S and the number of blocks by r. The original cap must be attained somewhere, since otherwise isometrically deleting one minimum vertex gives an admissible proper minor.

Deletion of S is isometric. Each selected vertex u is individually isometrically deletable. For any two nonadjacent neighbors a,b, we have d(a)+d(b)≥b(u)+k, while d(u)+e(u)<b(u)+k+q by exclusion of the first outcome. The earlier local wedge bound forces a common neighbor other than u. For an edge block uv, after deleting u the graph G' still satisfies the even cap k, and v has degree d(v)−1. A common neighbor of u,v has original degree at least k+1 by exclusion of the second outcome. Therefore for any two nonadjacent G'-neighbors a,b of v we still have d_G'(a)+d_G'(b)≥b(v)+k: any common neighbor exceeding k originally retains degree at least k, and the possible low vertex, if still a neighbor of v, has kept its degree because it could not have been common to u,v. Hence

    d_G'(v)+e_G'(v)−d_G'(a)−d_G'(b)≤q−2,

where e counts positive even witnesses. The definition of q gives n−1>3k+1+k(q−2). The local Ore bound implies a,b have a second common neighbor besides v, for otherwise j≥1. We can thus delete v isometrically from G'. The detours certifying deletion of each single vertex within its block use only vertices within distance two of that center in G. Every other block leaves them intact. This proves simultaneous isometry.

A survivor has neighbors in at most one block. If it loses two neighbors, those neighbors are the ends of an edge block and the survivor originally had degree at least k+1. All surviving degrees except possibly the one originally low vertex are thus at least k−1. Every surviving vertex now has at most k−1 positive even witnesses by the coverage property and isometry. Hence G−S qualifies, and it has a spanning path P by minimality.

## Recovering the few extra vertices when necessary

Closed neighborhoods of vertices chosen one per block are disjoint, so δ+1+(r−1)(k+1)≤n and

    r≤1+(k+floor((k+1)²/4))/(k+1)<(k+1)/4+2.

Consequently r≤h, |S|≤2r≤h+4, and we need enlarge P by at most three vertices to obtain a forbidden path missing at most h+1. Write p=max(0,|S|−h−1). We have p≤r−1, so can skip the one possible block containing the low vertex. When k=6, the bound r≤3 gives p≤2.

Each block containing only high vertices permits gaining at least one vertex locally on the original path P. A member x is directly insertable if it sees a P endpoint or two consecutive P points. Its addition then uses one or two contact positions. For a singleton x at least k contacts lie on P. If x were not directly insertable, all contact successors and predecessors would be at distance two from x, and at least k+1 distinct such positions would exist: in each maximal run of contacts separated by two steps the shifted positions outnumber the contacts by one. Thus every singleton is directly insertable.

For an edge block xy, use a direct insertion if either endpoint permits one. Otherwise each endpoint has at least k−1 contacts on P, with no endpoint or consecutive contacts. The same shift count gives exactly k−1 contacts forming one two-step run, and all k shifted positions exhaust that endpoint's even witnesses. If x and y have different contact sets, a contact belonging to y alone has distance two from x via y; it must be one of x's shifted positions. Two consecutive P contacts of x and y now accept insertion of the entire xy edge. This again uses just two contact positions. If the contact sets agree, they form a single run of k−1 alternating positions. Any of the k−2 interior midpoints of this run can be replaced by x,y between its two adjacent contacts, increasing the path order by one. Call these the midpoint choices.

Choose up to p ordinary insertions on distinct all-high blocks, preferring these to midpoint replacements. Different blocks' contact sets on the original P are disjoint, so these ordinary insertions have disjoint supporting positions. If further blocks are needed, greedily choose the earliest available midpoint among all remaining all-high blocks and exclude its three supporting positions from subsequent operations. Each ordinary support occupies at most two consecutive original positions and conflicts with at most two of a given block's midpoint choices, since midpoints are spaced by two. At a greedy midpoint step, all still-available alternatives are at least as late as the chosen midpoint. Of any other block's available midpoints at most two lie within two positions of that midpoint, so at most two alternatives are lost. Thus any as-yet-unused all-high block retains at least k−2−2(p−1)>0 choices if it requires a midpoint. The strict inequality holds for k≥7 since p≤3, and for k=6 since p≤2. There are enough all-high blocks since p≤r−1. These choices permit all the modifications simultaneously on P. The resulting path gains at least p vertices, misses at most h+1, and contradicts the universal gap in possible longest-path orders. This proves the dichotomy.
